# Maps that keep the laws

**Level:** 201 · for anyone who has met the definition of a linear map

**One line:** A linear map satisfies T(u + v) = Tu + Tv and T(av) = aTv; len(s + t) = len(s) + len(t), det(AB) = det(A) det(B) and 2^(a + b) = 2ᵃ · 2ᵇ have the same shape, one proof about that shape gives 2⁰ = 1, log 1 = 0, T(0) = 0 and det I = 1 all at once, and a map of this kind that can be undone shows two sets are one structure in two notations.

## Laws between two sets

The first three lessons were about laws inside one set. Chapter 3 of Axler's *Linear Algebra Done Right* begins with a law **between** two sets. A linear map T from V to W has to satisfy:

> **additivity** T(u + v) = Tu + Tv for all u, v ∈ V.
>
> **homogeneity** T(λv) = λ(Tv) for all λ ∈ F and all v ∈ V.

Read additivity as a promise about order: adding and then mapping gives the same answer as mapping and then adding. The map carries the operations of V over to W without disturbing them. The program checks five maps against the two conditions, then finds the same shape in places that have nothing to do with vectors.

## Five maps

Section 2 has three maps that pass and two that fail:

- **Multiply by i**, (x, y) ↦ (−y, x), is linear. It is the quarter turn from [multiplication rotates](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md).
- **Flatten**, (x, y) ↦ (x, 0), is linear too, even though it throws the second coordinate away. Linear does not mean invertible.
- **log₂** is linear, from [lesson 2](../a_definition_is_a_test/README.md)'s positive numbers (where "u + v" is u·v and "a times v" is vᵃ) to the ordinary numbers. Its two conditions are the laws of logarithms: log(uv) = log u + log v is additivity, and log(vᵃ) = a log v is homogeneity. The logarithm laws say exactly that the logarithm is a linear map.
- **x ↦ x + 1** is not linear, although its graph is a straight line. In linear algebra, a linear map always sends 0 to 0, because T(0) = T(0 + 0) = T(0) + T(0), and subtracting T(0) from both sides leaves T(0) = 0. This map sends 0 to 1. A straight line that misses the origin has its own name, *affine*.
- **x ↦ x²** fails both conditions, as the witnesses show.

## The same shape everywhere

Section 3 finds the same equation, f(a · b) = f(a) ∗ f(b), with other operations on each side. A map of this shape is a **homomorphism**, from Greek for "same shape", and a linear map is the vector-space kind.

- **len**, from strings under concatenation to whole numbers under +. It also explains [lesson 1](../laws_of_an_operation/README.md)'s finding that strings have no inverses. If s + t is the empty string, then len(s) + len(t) = 0, so both were empty to begin with.
- **n ↦ 2ⁿ**, from integers under + to fractions under ×. The exponent law 2^(a + b) = 2ᵃ · 2ᵇ is the statement that this map is a homomorphism.
- **det**, from 2 × 2 matrices under × to numbers under ×.
- **Squared length**, from complex numbers under × to numbers under ×: |zw|² = |z|²|w|². This is the "multiply the lengths" half of [multiplication rotates](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md). Written out in coordinates, it is the sums-of-two-squares identity in [multiplication as pairs](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_as_pairs/README.md).
- **M**, (x, y) ↦ [[x, −y], [y, x]], from complex numbers to 2 × 2 matrices. It keeps both multiplication and addition.

The last row fails, and that is the point of it. Squared length keeps multiplication but not addition: |z + w|² is not |z|² + |w|². A homomorphism always keeps some *particular* operation, and a map can keep one operation and not another.

## One proof, four books

Here is a theorem about every homomorphism between groups. Write e for the identity. Then f(e) = f(e · e) = f(e) ∗ f(e), and cancelling one f(e) leaves **f(e) is the identity**. Apply the same move to a · a⁻¹ = e, and it gives **f(a⁻¹) = f(a)⁻¹**. That is one proof, a line long, and here is what it says in four different courses:

| Homomorphism | From → to | Identity goes to identity | Inverse goes to inverse |
|---|---|---|---|
| n ↦ 2ⁿ | integers, + → nonzero fractions, × | 2⁰ = 1 | 2⁻ⁿ = 1/2ⁿ |
| log | positive numbers, × → numbers, + | log 1 = 0 | log(1/v) = −log v |
| linear map T | V, + → W, + | T(0) = 0 | T(−v) = −Tv |
| det | invertible matrices, × → nonzero numbers, × | det I = 1 | det(A⁻¹) = 1/det A |

A school book states 2⁰ = 1, a precalculus book proves log 1 = 0, a linear-algebra book proves T(0) = 0, and a later chapter proves det I = 1. Each is the same line with different names. This is the most direct answer to the question the chapter opened with. The repetition is real, and it goes away as soon as the shared shape has a name.

## A map that can be undone

Section 4 runs log₂ and n ↦ 2ⁿ back to back, and each undoes the other. A homomorphism with an inverse is an **isomorphism**, and it says two structures are the same one with the members renamed. Multiplying 8 by 1/4 among the positive numbers is the same calculation as adding 3 and −2 among the ordinary numbers, translated there and back.

That is why lesson 2's strange space passed the vector-space test: it is ℝ in disguise. It is also how a slide rule works. Its scales are marked at distances proportional to logarithms, so sliding one against the other adds lengths and reads off a product. A slide rule is an isomorphism made of wood.

Isomorphism is the second way mathematics avoids repeating itself. A theorem proved in one notation holds in every isomorphic one. Axler proves that two finite-dimensional vector spaces over the same field are isomorphic exactly when they have the same dimension. So in a sense there is only one vector space of each dimension, however many ways books find to write it. The same goes for the complex numbers. M is an isomorphism from the pairs of [03_Complex_Numbers](../../03_Complex_Numbers/README.md) onto the matrices [[x, −y], [y, x]]. The textbook that builds ℂ from pairs and the one that builds it from matrices built the same thing, and neither is the real one.

## Products of linear maps

Axler goes on to list the algebraic properties of products of linear maps, where ST means "S after T": associativity, identity, and two distributive properties. Section 5 checks them on a quarter turn, a flattening and a shear, and sorts them by where they come from:

- **Associativity and identity are true for any functions at all.** (ST)U and S(TU) both send v to S(T(U(v))), so there is nothing linear about the reason. Matrix multiplication is associative *because* a matrix is a linear map and matrix multiplication is composition. A book that proves associativity for matrices by expanding the triple sum is doing work that composition already did.
- **(S + T)U = SU + TU** is also true for any functions. Both sides are S(U(v)) + T(U(v)).
- **S(T + U) = ST + SU** needs S to be additive. With S = (x, y) ↦ (x + 1, y) the program finds it failing.
- **Commutativity is missing.** Turning after flattening is not flattening after turning.

With addition, those are the laws of a ring, so the linear maps from a space to itself form a ring, and not a commutative one. That is the 2 × 2 matrix row of [lesson 1](../laws_of_an_operation/README.md#two-operations-and-the-law-that-links-them), seen from the other side.

## The general topic: categories

Look at the first two laws of section 5 again: composition is associative, and there are identity maps. Those two laws, and nothing else, are the definition of a **category**: a collection of objects, arrows between them, and a way to compose arrows that is associative and has identities. Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane gave the definition in 1945.

Sets with functions form a category. So do groups with homomorphisms, vector spaces with linear maps, and ordered sets with order-preserving maps. A theorem proved about categories holds in all of them, and **category theory** is the subject that studies the pattern this chapter keeps finding: structures, and the maps that keep them.
