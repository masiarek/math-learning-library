# The identity laws: A ∪ ∅ = A and A ∩ U = A

**Level:** 101 · one law of [the algebra of sets](../../README.md), on its own page

**One line:** Adding nothing changes nothing, and keeping everything keeps A: ∅ is the identity for ∪ and U for ∩, the way 0 is for + and 1 for ×.

## The law

| Sets | Logic | Python |
|---|---|---|
| A ∪ ∅ = A, A ∩ U = A | p ∨ false = p, p ∧ true = p | `a | set() == a and a & U == a` |

## Why it is true

For any x, "x ∈ A or x ∈ ∅" is just "x ∈ A", since x ∈ ∅ is always false; and "x ∈ A and x ∈ U" is just "x ∈ A", since x ∈ U is always true inside the universe. In logic, *or false* and *and true* both leave p alone. A law of sets is decided one member at a time, so checking it on every subset of a four-element universe, every pattern of membership there is, proves it; the program does that and prints the matching truth table.

## Where it is used, and the trap

The identity elements are what make the subsets of U a [monoid](../../../../06_Algebraic_Structures/laws_of_an_operation/README.md) under ∪ and again under ∩, and in code `a | set()` and `a & U` are the no-ops. The trap is the second: A ∩ U = A only because U was fixed first; with a bigger U the complement changes, though this law does not.
