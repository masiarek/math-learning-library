# Mean, average, arithmetic mean

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who was taught to add them up and divide by how many

**One line:** In a classroom, *average*, *mean* and *arithmetic mean* all name one calculation, the sum divided by the count, but they are three sizes of word rather than three synonyms: *average* is any single value that stands for a set, *mean* is one family of averages, and *arithmetic* picks out the member of that family that adds.

## One calculation, a pile of names

A typical school definition reads: *the mean, also called the arithmetic average, is a calculated central value of a set of numbers; to find it, add all the numbers, then divide the sum by how many there are.* That sentence uses two names, and the word everyone actually says, *average*, is a third. Keep reading and more turn up:

| Name | Where you meet it |
|---|---|
| average | everyday speech, the news, and the spreadsheet function `AVERAGE` |
| mean | statistics books, and Python's `statistics.mean` |
| arithmetic mean, arithmetic average | wherever another kind of mean is also in play |
| x̄, read "x-bar" | the mean of a sample of data |
| μ, the Greek letter mu | the mean of a whole population |
| `AVG` | SQL |
| expected value, E[X] | probability: the long-run mean of a quantity that depends on chance |

On a plain list of numbers every one of them is the same calculation: add them up, divide by how many. Section 1 of the program hands five test scores to Python's `statistics.mean` and to SQL's `AVG`, and both return the 78 that adding and dividing gives by hand.

So why so many names? There are two reasons, and they are worth keeping apart.

**Everyday word, technical word.** *Average* is the word of ordinary speech: average height, average temperature, the average commute. *Mean* is the word mathematicians and statisticians use for the same thing. Neither is more correct; they belong to different registers, the way *total* and *sum* do.

**More than one mean.** *Mean* is not a single calculation. There are others, the geometric mean and the harmonic mean among them, and each is the right answer to a different question. The adjective *arithmetic* names the one the school definition describes, the one that adds. It exists for the moments when another mean is in the room, and in a first course on averages no other mean ever is. That is why a textbook can introduce "arithmetic average" as a synonym and never explain the adjective: in that classroom it has nothing to tell apart.

There is one more fact that the usual explanation leaves out, and it is the one that can actually mislead you: **"average" is a wider word than "mean".** In statistics the median and the mode are averages too, and English schools, for one, teach the mean, the median and the mode as three kinds of average. So *average* is not only the everyday word for the mean. Sometimes it names a different number altogether.

## Three sizes of word

Put the names in order of size and each one sits inside the one before it:

```text
average            any single value that stands for the whole set
├── median         the middle value
├── mode           the most common value
└── mean           keeps a total unchanged; the adjective says which total
    ├── arithmetic     keeps the sum                 ← "the average" in school
    ├── geometric      keeps the product
    ├── harmonic       keeps the sum of reciprocals
    └── quadratic      keeps the sum of squares
```

The school definition sits at the bottom of the tree, on the arithmetic mean. In a classroom where nothing else on the tree is ever used, every name above it points at the same number. The names come apart as soon as you meet a second branch.

## What makes a mean a mean

Section 2 is what makes the adjectives make sense. Replace every score by 78 and the total is still 390. That is the arithmetic mean, put exactly: **the one number that can stand in for every value without changing the sum.** The definition's "add, then divide" is just how you find that number.

It also explains the definition's other two words. *Central*: because the total is kept, the distances above the mean and below it cancel exactly, −6 + 7 + 12 − 14 + 1 = 0, so the mean is the balance point of the data. Put five equal weights on a ruler at 72, 85, 90, 64 and 79 and it balances at 78. *Calculated*: nobody scored 78. The mean is worked out from the list, not picked from it, and it need not be one of the numbers at all.

Every other mean keeps a different total, and the adjective says which one:

| Mean | Keeps unchanged | Of two numbers a and b | Right for |
|---|---|---|---|
| arithmetic | the sum | (a + b) / 2 | amounts that add: scores, salaries, lengths |
| geometric | the product | √(ab) | rates that multiply: growth, interest, inflation |
| harmonic | the sum of reciprocals | 2 / (1/a + 1/b) | rates over equal amounts: speeds over equal distances |
| quadratic, or root mean square | the sum of squares | √((a² + b²) / 2) | sizes that matter squared: the rated value of an AC voltage |

On the same two numbers they disagree. For 2 and 8 the arithmetic mean is 5, the geometric mean 4 and the harmonic mean 3.2. For positive numbers that is always the order, arithmetic ≥ geometric ≥ harmonic, with equality only when all the numbers are equal. So using the arithmetic mean where the geometric or harmonic mean belongs is wrong in a predictable direction: it always comes out too high.

## When the adjective matters

**Growth multiplies** (section 3). An investment doubles one year and halves the next. The returns are +100% and −50%, and their arithmetic mean says +25% a year, which would turn 1,000 into 1,562.50. The account holds 1,000, exactly where it started. Returns are applied by multiplying, so the mean that describes them has to keep the product of the growth factors: √(2 × ½) = 1, a growth factor of 1, which is 0% a year. This is why investors quote a compound annual growth rate, which is a geometric mean, and why an arithmetic mean of returns overstates what the money did whenever the returns are not all equal.

**Speed divides** (section 4). Drive 60 km at 30 km/h and the 60 km back at 60 km/h. The arithmetic mean of the two speeds is 45 km/h, but the trip took 2 hours plus 1 hour for 120 km, which is 40 km/h. Time is distance divided by speed, so the mean that keeps the total time has to keep the sum of the reciprocals of the speeds: the harmonic mean, 2 / (1/30 + 1/60) = 40. You spend twice as long on the slow leg as on the fast one, and the arithmetic mean counts the two legs as if they took equal time.

In both cases the arithmetic mean was calculated correctly. It answered a question nobody asked.

## When "average" misleads

Section 5 is where the loose word does damage. Ten people work at a small firm; nine earn between 32,000 and 45,000, and the owner takes 400,000. Three different numbers can each honestly be called "the average salary":

- the **mean**, 73,500, is the payroll shared out equally. Nine of the ten earn less than it.
- the **median**, 37,000, is the middle: half earn less, half earn more.
- the **mode**, 34,000, is the salary the most people are paid.

None of them is wrong. They answer different questions: *what would each person get if the payroll were shared equally?* and *what does a typical person here earn?* One large value drags the mean a long way and leaves the median where it was. That is why official income figures are so often medians; the headline figure from the US Census Bureau, for example, is *median* household income. When a sentence says "the average" and the numbers are lopsided, as incomes, house prices and waiting times usually are, ask which average it means.

## Which word to use

- **"Average"** is fine in everyday writing, as long as nobody could reasonably ask "which one?".
- **"Mean"** is the word to use when the median is also in play, or when the data are lopsided and the difference matters.
- **"Arithmetic mean"** is worth its extra word only when a geometric or harmonic mean is also in the room. Anywhere else the adjective has nothing to tell apart, and that is exactly why it sounds like a mystery.

## In software

The tools use the same names, and mostly agree on them. A spreadsheet's `AVERAGE` is the arithmetic mean, and `GEOMEAN`, `HARMEAN`, `MEDIAN` and `MODE` sit beside it. Python's `statistics` module has `mean`, `geometric_mean`, `harmonic_mean`, `median` and `mode`. SQL has only `AVG`, the arithmetic mean. NumPy has both `numpy.mean` and `numpy.average`, and they compute the same thing unless you give `average` a list of weights, which `mean` does not accept.
