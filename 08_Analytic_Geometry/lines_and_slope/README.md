# Lines: slope, equations, parallel and perpendicular

**Level:** 101 · for anyone starting precalculus

**One line:** A line has one slope, the same whichever two of its points you measure it with, because any two make similar right triangles; every equation of a line is that fact rearranged, parallel lines share it, and perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is −1 because a quarter turn sends (run, rise) to (−rise, run).

## The idea

Pick two points on a non-vertical line, (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂). Going from the first to the second, the **run** is x₂ − x₁ and the **rise** is y₂ − y₁, and the **slope** is

> **m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)**, rise over run.

The fact that makes this one number, not a number per pair of points, is geometry: any two points of the line, with the corner below one and beside the other, make a right triangle, and all those triangles have the same angles, so they are [similar](../../10_Geometry/congruent_and_similar_triangles/README.md) and share the ratio of their legs. The program checks ten pairs of points on one line and gets 2/3 ten times.

Once the slope is one number, the equation of the line follows. A point (x, y) is on the line through (x₁, y₁) with slope m exactly when its slope from (x₁, y₁) is m: (y − y₁)/(x − x₁) = m. Multiply up and that is the **point-slope form**, y − y₁ = m(x − x₁). Everything else in the section is this equation rearranged.

This page follows section 1.3, *Lines*, in Michael Sullivan's *Precalculus*.

## The forms of the equation

| Form | Equation | What you read off |
|---|---|---|
| point-slope | y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) | a point and the slope: the form to *write* first |
| slope-intercept | y = mx + b | the slope m and the y-intercept b: the form to *graph* |
| general | Ax + By = C, A and B not both 0 | the intercepts, by setting x = 0 or y = 0; the only form that includes vertical lines |
| horizontal | y = b | slope 0 |
| vertical | x = a | slope undefined |

## What the slope says, and what "undefined" means

Positive slope rises from left to right; negative falls; 0 is horizontal. A slope of 3 means up 3 for every 1 across; a slope of 1/3, up 1 for every 3 across. For a vertical line the run is 0 and rise/0 is not a number: the slope is **undefined**. That is not the same as "zero slope", which is the horizontal line, and it is not "infinite" either: there is simply nothing to compute. A vertical line has no equation of the form y = mx + b, only x = a.

**The order of the points.** Either point may be first, as long as both differences start from the same one: (−3 − 2)/(5 − 1) and (2 − (−3))/(1 − 5) are both −5/4. Mixing, (−3 − 2)/(1 − 5), gives +5/4, the wrong sign. The [distance formula](../distance_formula/README.md) forgave mixing because it squares; slope does not.

## Parallel and perpendicular

**Parallel** lines, which never meet, have the same slope and different intercepts. The same slope and the same intercept is not two parallel lines but one line written twice.

**Perpendicular** lines, which meet at a right angle, have slopes whose product is −1: each is the **negative reciprocal** of the other, so 2 and −1/2, or −5/2 and 2/5. Why: turning a step of (run, rise) = (a, b) a quarter turn gives (−b, a), the rule that [multiplying by i](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md) follows. The slopes are b/a and a/(−b), and their product is −1. The program also checks each right angle the old way, with the [converse of Pythagoras](../../10_Geometry/pythagorean_theorem/README.md). The rule needs both slopes to exist: a horizontal line and a vertical line are perpendicular, but the vertical one has no slope to multiply.

## Questions

**1. Find the slope of the line through (2, 3) and (−4, 5), and interpret it.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(5 − 3)/(−4 − 2) = 2/(−6) = −1/3. The line falls 1 unit for every 3 units to the right.

</details>

**2. Find the slope of the line through (3, −1) and (3, 7). Of the line through (−2, 4) and (3, 4).**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

First: run 3 − 3 = 0, so undefined: the vertical line x = 3. Second: rise 0, so slope 0: the horizontal line y = 4.

</details>

**3. Write the equation of the line with slope 4 through (1, 2), in point-slope and slope-intercept forms.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

y − 2 = 4(x − 1). Distribute and add 2: y = 4x − 2.

</details>

**4. Write the line through (1, 2) and (5, −3) in general form.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

m = −5/4. y − 2 = −(5/4)(x − 1); times 4: 4y − 8 = −5x + 5; so 5x + 4y = 13. Check with (5, −3): 25 − 12 = 13.

</details>

**5. Find the slope and y-intercept of 2x − 3y = 6. Then its x-intercept.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Solve for y: −3y = −2x + 6, y = (2/3)x − 2. Slope 2/3, y-intercept −2. y = 0 gives x = 3.

</details>

**6. Find the line parallel to y = 2x + 1 through (3, 4), and the line perpendicular to it through (3, 4).**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Parallel: slope 2, y − 4 = 2(x − 3), so y = 2x − 2. Perpendicular: slope −1/2, y − 4 = −½(x − 3), so y = −½x + 11/2.

</details>

**7. Are y = 3x − 1 and 6x − 2y = 2 parallel?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The second is y = 3x − 1: the same line. Same slope and same intercept means one line, not two parallel ones.

</details>

**8. What is the slope of a line perpendicular to x = 5?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

0: the perpendicular lines are horizontal, y = b. The product rule cannot be used, because x = 5 has no slope; this is the case it excludes.

</details>

**9. Using slopes, show that (1, 1), (4, 3) and (7, 5) lie on one line.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Slope from the first to the second: 2/3. From the second to the third: 2/3. Equal slopes through a shared point mean the same line.

</details>

**10. Using slopes, show that A = (−2, 1), B = (2, 3), C = (3, 1) make a right triangle.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Slope AB = 2/4 = 1/2, slope BC = −2/1 = −2. Product −1, so AB ⊥ BC and the right angle is at B, which is what the [distance formula lesson](../distance_formula/README.md) found with the converse of Pythagoras.

</details>

**11. Why is slope the same for every pair of points on a line?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Each pair makes a right triangle whose legs are the run and the rise and whose hypotenuse lies along the line. All those triangles have the same angles, so they are similar, and similar triangles have the same ratio of corresponding sides: rise/run.

</details>

**12. The negative reciprocal of 2/3 is −3/2. Why does a perpendicular line flip the fraction, not just the sign?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A quarter turn swaps the roles of run and rise: the step (3, 2) becomes (−2, 3). Its slope is 3/(−2). Changing only the sign would give a mirror image, −2/3, which is not at right angles.

</details>

## How to practise

1. **Start every "find the line" problem in point-slope form.** It needs a point and a slope, and nothing else; rearrange afterwards.
2. **Check with a second point.** After writing a line through two points, put the other point in. One substitution catches most mistakes.
3. **Keep the order of subtraction.** Write the two differences one under the other, both starting from the same point.
4. **Check the program**, and do the cards.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Nachylenie prostej to przyrost y przez przyrost x: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). Jest jedną liczbą dla całej prostej, bo dowolne dwa jej punkty tworzą trójkąt prostokątny o tych samych kątach, a trójkąty podobne mają ten sam stosunek boków. Z tego jednego faktu wynikają wszystkie postacie równania: punktowo-kierunkowa y − y₁ = m(x − x₁), kierunkowa y = mx + b (b to punkt przecięcia z osią y) i ogólna Ax + By = C, jedyna, która obejmuje prostą pionową x = a. Prosta pionowa nie ma nachylenia: dzielenie przez zero, nie „zero" ani „nieskończoność".

Proste równoległe mają równe nachylenia; prostopadłe mają nachylenia o iloczynie −1, bo obrót o ćwierć obrotu zamienia krok (a, b) na (−b, a). Pułapki: przy odejmowaniu obie różnice muszą zaczynać się od tego samego punktu; to samo nachylenie i ten sam wyraz wolny to jedna prosta, nie dwie równoległe. Program sprawdza to na dokładnych ułamkach.
