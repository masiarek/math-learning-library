# The distance formula

**Level:** 101 · for anyone starting precalculus

**One line:** The distance formula is the Pythagorean theorem with the legs read off the coordinates, and its squares do a second job: they throw away the signs, which is why the order of the points never matters and no absolute value is needed.

## The idea

Two points, P₁ = (x₁, y₁) and P₂ = (x₂, y₂). How far apart are they? Add a third point, P₃ = (x₂, y₁): it has P₂'s x and P₁'s y, so it sits level with P₁ and directly below (or above) P₂. The three points make a right triangle with the right angle at P₃. Its horizontal leg is |x₂ − x₁| long and its vertical leg |y₂ − y₁|, because a horizontal distance is a difference of x-coordinates and a vertical one a difference of y-coordinates. [Pythagoras](../../10_Geometry/pythagorean_theorem/README.md) does the rest:

> **d(P₁, P₂) = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)**

In words: subtract the x's and square, subtract the y's and square, add, take the square root.

The book's Example 1 is exactly this: from (1, 3) to (5, 6), the corner is (5, 3), the legs are 4 and 3, and the distance is 5. Written out as a proof, ten lines each with its reason and the horizontal and vertical pairs as cases of their own, the same argument is the worked example of [what a proof is](../../11_Logic/what_a_proof_is/README.md).

This page follows the opening of section 1.1, *The Distance and Midpoint Formulas*, in Michael Sullivan's *Precalculus*, which sends the reader back to [Appendix A.2](../../10_Geometry/README.md) for the Pythagorean theorem first.

## Symbol by symbol

| Piece | Say it | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| P₁, P₂ | "P one, P two" | two points, named with subscripts so that their coordinates can be x₁, y₁ and x₂, y₂. The subscript is a label, not a power. |
| d(P₁, P₂) | "the distance from P one to P two" | a number, never negative. The notation reads like a function of two points, and it is one. |
| x₂ − x₁ | "x two minus x one" | the horizontal change, which may be negative (moving left). |
| (x₂ − x₁)² | | its square, which is never negative. |
| √ | "square root" | the non-negative root: d is a distance. |

## What the squares are doing

Two things at once. They are Pythagoras, the squares on the legs. And they erase direction: x₂ − x₁ and x₁ − x₂ are negatives of each other, and squaring cannot tell them apart. Three consequences follow, and each is a small theorem:

- **Order does not matter.** d(P₁, P₂) = d(P₂, P₁), so you can call either point "1".
- **No absolute values needed**, though the legs are |x₂ − x₁| and |y₂ − y₁|: |t|² = t² for every t.
- **Distance from the origin.** Put P₁ = (0, 0): the distance of a point (x, y) from the origin is √(x² + y²), the formula with nothing to subtract. It is the length of the arrow from the origin to the point, called the [length of the vector](../distance_in_n_dimensions/README.md) (x, y) in linear algebra and the absolute value |x + yi| in [the complex numbers](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md); exercise 3 below works it. Axler states this special case on its own line, the other books leave it to the reader.
- **Horizontal and vertical segments are not special cases.** If y₁ = y₂, the formula gives √((x₂ − x₁)²) = |x₂ − x₁|, the absolute value, because √(t²) = |t|, not t. The one-dimensional distance on a number line, |b − a|, is this formula with one coordinate.

## Exact answers

d² is always exact when the coordinates are whole numbers or fractions. d is exact only when d² is a perfect square, which the book arranges in its examples and life does not. Otherwise the answer is left as a square root in simplest form, √20 = √(4 · 5) = 2√5, and a decimal only at the end. Better still: **to compare distances, compare their squares.** Squaring keeps order among non-negative numbers, so d(A, B) < d(A, C) exactly when d(A, B)² < d(A, C)², and no square root is ever taken. This is how the book's next example decides whether three points make a right triangle: by the [converse of the Pythagorean theorem](../../10_Geometry/pythagorean_theorem/README.md), using squared distances only.

Section 6 is the triangle inequality, seen from the coordinates: the straight path from P to R is never longer than a detour through Q, and it is equal only when Q lies on the segment. It gives a test for three points being on one line that needs no slope.

## Questions

**1. Find the distance between (−4, 5) and (3, 2).**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(3 − (−4))² + (2 − 5)² = 49 + 9 = 58, so d = √58 ≈ 7.62. 58 = 2 · 29 has no square factor, so √58 is already simplest.

</details>

**2. Someone computes the distance from (2, 7) to (5, 3) as √((2 − 5)² + (7 − 3)²). The other person uses (5 − 2) and (3 − 7). Who is right?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both, and they get the same √(9 + 16) = 5: each difference only changes sign, and the square erases the sign. Even a mixed order, (5 − 2) with (7 − 3), would survive here, but only because of the squares. Keep one order anyway: slope, two sections on, divides one difference by the other, and a mixed order there flips the sign of the answer.

</details>

**3. Find the distance between (0, 0) and (a, b).**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

√(a² + b²). This is the distance of a point from the origin, and in [the complex numbers](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md) it is the absolute value |a + bi|.

</details>

**4. Why is √((x₂ − x₁)²) equal to |x₂ − x₁| and not x₂ − x₁?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The square root sign means the non-negative root. If x₂ − x₁ = −6, its square is 36 and √36 = 6 = |−6|. √(t²) = |t| for every real t.

</details>

**5. Is (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² the same as (x₂ − x₁ + y₂ − y₁)²? Test on (0, 0) and (3, 4).**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No: 9 + 16 = 25 against (3 + 4)² = 49. Squaring does not split over a sum. Each difference is squared on its own.

</details>

**6. Is √(a² + b²) = a + b? Test with a = 3, b = 4.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No: √25 = 5, not 7. The square root does not split over a sum either. This is the most common algebra error in the whole chapter.

</details>

**7. Do A = (−2, 1), B = (2, 3), C = (3, 1) form a right triangle? Where is the right angle?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Squared distances: AB² = 16 + 4 = 20, BC² = 1 + 4 = 5, AC² = 25 + 0 = 25. The longest is AC², and 20 + 5 = 25, so yes. The right angle is at B, opposite the longest side AC. No square root was needed.

</details>

**8. Which point is closer to the origin, (5, −1) or (−3, 4)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Compare squares: 25 + 1 = 26 against 9 + 16 = 25. (−3, 4) is closer, by a hair. √ was never needed.

</details>

**9. Find all points on the x-axis at distance 5 from (1, 3).**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A point on the x-axis is (x, 0). (x − 1)² + (0 − 3)² = 25 gives (x − 1)² = 16, so x − 1 = ±4: x = 5 or x = −3. Two points, (5, 0) and (−3, 0), as a circle of radius 5 around (1, 3) would cut the axis twice.

</details>

**10. Are (0, 0), (3, 4) and (6, 8) on one line? Use distances only.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

d(first, second) = 5, d(second, third) = 5, d(first, third) = 10. The two short ones add up to the long one exactly, so the middle point is on the segment: yes. If the sum were bigger, the three would make a real triangle.

</details>

**11. The distance formula uses the Pythagorean theorem. Where, exactly, is the right angle?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

At P₃ = (x₂, y₁), where a horizontal line through P₁ meets a vertical line through P₂. The axes are perpendicular, so horizontal and vertical lines are too. On a graph whose axes are not at right angles, the formula would be wrong.

</details>

**12. What does the formula give when P₁ = P₂? What does that say about the distance being zero?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

0. And conversely, a sum of two squares is 0 only when both are 0, so d = 0 only when the points are the same point.

</details>

## How to practise

1. **Do the corner first.** For the first ten problems, write P₃ = (x₂, y₁) and the two legs before using the formula. After that, skip it, but you will know why it works.
2. **Signs in brackets.** Write (3 − (−4)), with the brackets, every time a coordinate is negative. Most wrong answers are a lost minus sign.
3. **Squares to compare, roots to report.** Decide "which is closer" and "is it right-angled" with d²; take the root only when a length is asked for.
4. **Check with the program**, and do the cards, `trap` tag first.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Wzór na odległość dwóch punktów to twierdzenie Pitagorasa zapisane we współrzędnych. Trzeci punkt, (x₂, y₁), leży na tej samej wysokości co pierwszy i dokładnie pod drugim, więc trzy punkty tworzą trójkąt prostokątny. Przyprostokątne to różnice współrzędnych, |x₂ − x₁| i |y₂ − y₁|, a odległość to przeciwprostokątna: d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²).

Kwadraty robią tu dwie rzeczy: są Pitagorasem i kasują znaki. Dlatego kolejność punktów nie ma znaczenia i nie potrzeba wartości bezwzględnej; √(t²) = |t|, a nie t. Najczęstsze błędy: zgubiony minus przy ujemnej współrzędnej i „pierwiastek z sumy to suma pierwiastków", co jest nieprawdą: √(9 + 16) = 5, nie 7. Żeby porównać odległości, wystarczy porównać ich kwadraty i pierwiastka w ogóle nie liczyć. Program sprawdza to wszystko na dokładnych liczbach i liczy odległość dowolnych dwóch punktów podanych w wierszu poleceń.
