# 12_Learning_to_Learn — how do you know that you know?

**Level:** 101 · for anyone using this library, or any other, to learn mathematics

Every other chapter here is about mathematics. This one is about the person reading it: how to tell whether you have learned a page, how to learn it so that it stays, and why the pages come with questions and flashcards. It follows Saundra McGuire's *Teach Yourself How to Learn*, chapters 3 and 4, with five lessons and four summaries from Todd Zakrajsek's *The New Science of Learning*, and like every chapter it backs each claim with a program. It needs nothing but school arithmetic, and one lesson uses the Pythagorean theorem.

## The through-line

A feeling of knowing is not knowing. Lesson 1 measures the gap between the two, with a score that rewards nothing but an honest guess. Lesson 2 shows that what you remember depends on what you did with the material, not on how long you looked at it. Lesson 3 names the levels of doing, from recalling a formula to building the formula that produces the facts, and lesson 4 keeps what you built, at a cost that grows only with the logarithm of how long you want it. Lesson 5 turns the same habit on arguments: a confident yes-or-no usually hides a cutoff on a smooth quantity, and naming the cutoff is where perspective taking starts. Lesson 6 turns it on belief in yourself: a belief low enough to stop you trying cuts off the only evidence that could change it, and small steps let the evidence back in. Lesson 7 applies lesson 1's check to time: an estimate is a prediction, its errors all point one way, and a recorded ratio and a reserved hour correct for them. Lesson 8 goes back inside the head: working memory holds about four chunks, a chunk is whatever practice has made automatic, and so the load of a page depends on its reader. Lesson 9 asks what a practice sheet rehearses: a blocked one lets you skip choosing the method, which is most of what a mixed exam tests. Each step is something a reader can check on themselves, which is the point: metacognition is the habit of checking.

## Beyond the lessons: self-regulation and self-efficacy

*The New Science of Learning*, chapter 2, defines *self-regulation* as the process that helps you meet your own goals: pick a target, plan how to reach it, and stick to the plan until you do. His example is a first-year student who plans an hour a day for each of four classes and is then invited on a weekend bike trip, and starts telling themselves they can study more next week. He names four parts, and three already have a lesson here:

- **Metacognition**, planning, acting, evaluating afterwards and adjusting: [lesson 1](metacognition/README.md), which measures the evaluating step.
- **Avoiding procrastination**, and the "I'll study more next week" it runs on: [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md) says why the deferred hours are worth less, since hours piled into one week are crammed rather than spaced.
- **Cognitive load and automaticity**: a step practised until it is automatic stops using up attention. [Cognitive load](cognitive_load/README.md) is the lesson.
- **Self-efficacy**, the belief that you can carry the plan out, which the paragraphs below take up; when it has collapsed after repeated failure, [learned helplessness](learned_helplessness/README.md) is the lesson.

His Study Tip 2.1 is the practical rule: **start as small as necessary, but develop a habit of completing whatever goal you set.** A goal of twenty minutes that is always met teaches the plan to be believed; a goal of four hours that is usually missed teaches the opposite. The same chapter's section on managing emotions (situation selection, attention deployment, situation modification, reappraisal, response modulation) is useful reading and not a lesson, for the reason motivation is not: no program can show it without invented data.

*Self-efficacy* is Albert Bandura's word (1997) for how far you believe you can succeed at a given task. It matters most when the task gets hard: someone who expects to succeed keeps going, and someone who does not gives up. The book lists Bandura's four sources of it:

1. **Performance outcomes**: having done something similar before, and succeeded. A failure on something similar makes it easy to give up.
2. **Vicarious experience**: watching someone you identify with do it. Study Tip 2.2: *if you don't know how to do something, ask someone to show you, so you can see someone be successful at the task.*
3. **Verbal persuasion**: "come on, I know you can do it" from someone you trust, and its harmful opposite, being told that people like you are not good at this.
4. **Physiological feedback**: a racing heart before an exam read as ordinary nerves raises it; read as proof you are unprepared, it lowers it.

The book is careful about one point: believing you can does not mean you can. Self-efficacy is a reason to *try*, and trying is what raises the chance of success. That is where [metacognition](metacognition/README.md) comes in. Self-efficacy is a belief about your chances before the task, and lesson 1 is about checking beliefs of that kind against results: low belief after real success is underconfidence, and it costs you the attempts you never make. The book's advice fits the rest of this chapter: take part in class, start small and work up (Study Tip 2.1 again), and watch people do the task well. Self-efficacy is not a lesson here for the same reason as motivation: no program can show a belief changing without invented data.

**Imposter syndrome** is the same miscalibration pointed the other way: a person who has succeeded but believes they are about to be found out. The book says three things about it. Nearly everyone feels this way at times, so feeling it means you are like everyone else, not that you do not belong. It is found much more often in underrepresented and marginalised groups, partly because of a double standard: the same mistake is put down to a hard situation for some people and to being unprepared for others. And the way out is to **listen to feedback and evidence**: material you can show you know, solutions others agree with, programs you finish. That is lesson 1's rule, a feeling of knowing is not knowing, turned round: a feeling of *not* knowing is not evidence either, and the record of results is.

## Beyond the lessons: goals and schedules

Chapter 3 of *The New Science of Learning*, "Developing Your Learning Strategy", starts from an observation: many students go to class, study, take tests and repeat, and that is not a plan. Its pieces, and where this library has a reason behind them:

- **Specific, challenging goals** beat easy goals and "do your best" (Locke and Latham). A good goal sits just below your best expected outcome, about 90% of it: an exam goal of 90% rather than 100%, which one missed question would break.
- **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, timely. "Do well in all my classes" fails the first two; "maintain a B− average on all assignments and exams" passes. Study Tip 3.1: set one SMART goal and one SMART *outcome*, what you will be able to do at the end, for every study session. An outcome such as "list the brain structures of the limbic system without notes" is a test you can take, which is what [metacognition](metacognition/README.md) asks for.
- **Process goals and end goals.** "Get an A in statistics" is an end goal; "study 3 hours a day, 5 days a week" is the process goal that gets you there, and the one people forget.
- **Goal creep**: a vague goal drifts. You study hard, do well on the first exams, skip a session, still do well, skip more, and finish the semester scrambling to pass. A measurable goal shows the drift while it is small.
- **Break large goals into smaller ones.** Short subgoals give feedback often, the [start small](learned_helplessness/README.md) remedy again.
- **Stretch goals**: a chance of success around 10%, still possible. Study Tip 3.2: set one for each exam. [Learned helplessness](learned_helplessness/README.md) explains why only one: a record made mostly of missed goals teaches you to stop trying, and the book's point is that a missed stretch goal still accomplishes a lot. *Do the math* on its example: a million steps in a school year of about 32 weeks is roughly 4,500 steps a day.
- **Build a schedule** with every deadline from every syllabus, study blocks treated like meetings, time for yourself, and **an hour in reserve every day**: [the buffer hour](the_buffer_hour/README.md) is why.
- **To-do lists**: write a task down as soon as you think of it; give it a date, then one extension, then ask whether it needs doing at all; do a two-minute task at once; review the list morning and evening; **estimate each item's time**, which [the buffer hour](the_buffer_hour/README.md) turns into a ratio you can correct; and put anything over 30 minutes straight into the calendar.
- **Organisation**: a task is done when its product is filed, not when you press send, as dinner is done when the dishes are put away.
- **Talking to faculty**: listen actively, summarise what you heard before you leave (10 to 15 seconds), ask before taking notes on a phone, and ask for clarification rather than pretend to understand. The last is lesson 1's rule again: saying "I understand" is a prediction, and the assignment is the test.

## Beyond the lessons: how learning and memory work

The whole path, as one line:

```
attention / encoding  ──>  working memory  ──>  consolidation  ──>  long-term storage
        │                        │                     │                     │
  acetylcholine             bottleneck            sleep / rest           re-retrieval
  (a focused beam)       (3–5 chunks at once)    (LTP and BDNF)     (the trace strengthens)
```

The bottom row names the biology usually given for each stage: acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter that sharpens attention; long-term potentiation (LTP), the lasting strengthening of a synapse that is used; and BDNF, a growth factor that supports it. This library cannot demonstrate any of that with a program, and does not try. What it can do is say what each stage means for how you study:

| Stage | What limits it | What to do | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| attention / encoding | what you did not attend to was never encoded | remove extraneous load; know the goal before you read | [cognitive load](cognitive_load/README.md), [count the vowels](count_the_vowels/README.md) |
| working memory | about four chunks at once | make the foundations automatic, so they take one place each | [cognitive load](cognitive_load/README.md) |
| consolidation | a new trace is weak, and the next class can overwrite it | review within the day; sleep instead of cramming | [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md) |
| long-term storage | a trace fades unless it is used | retrieve it, spaced out and mixed with other topics | [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md), [interleaving](interleaving/README.md) |

And beside every stage, [metacognition](metacognition/README.md): a test, not a feeling, says whether the trace is there.

### The "big four" techniques, and how sure each claim is

Summaries of the cognitive science of learning, including AI-written ones, tend to name the same four techniques, and this chapter has a reason behind three of them:

| Technique | What to do | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| active recall (retrieval practice) | close the book and write down what you remember; answer questions before checking notes | [metacognition](metacognition/README.md), [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md) |
| spaced repetition | review at growing intervals, for example day 1, 3, 7, 21, 60; let Anki schedule it | [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md) |
| interleaving | mix topics and problem types in one session | [interleaving](interleaving/README.md) |
| elaboration and dual coding | connect new ideas to what you know, in your own words and in a drawing; explain it simply, as if teaching (the Feynman technique) | [studying vs learning](studying_vs_learning/README.md): teach-the-material mode |

The same summaries then go further, and the further they go the weaker the evidence gets. Weighing a claim before you adopt it is [metacognition](metacognition/README.md) applied to advice:

| Claim | How sure |
|---|---|
| Testing yourself beats rereading; spacing beats cramming; interleaving helps with problems that are easy to confuse | **Well established**: many experiments in classrooms, over decades. |
| Sleep consolidates memory; cramming instead of sleeping costs twice | **Well established**, though which stage of sleep does what is still argued over. |
| Working memory holds about 3–5 chunks | **Well established** as an approximate figure (Cowan, 2001). |
| LTP, the lasting strengthening of a synapse that is used, is a cellular basis of memory; acetylcholine sharpens attention; dopamine makes plasticity easier | **Good evidence in animals**; how it adds up to studying better is not known in any detail. |
| Aerobic exercise raises BDNF | **True for blood levels just after exercise**; the effect on what you learn in the next hour is small and uncertain. |
| A quiet rest of 10–15 minutes after studying, with no phone, helps retention | **Some evidence** (wakeful rest after learning, Dewar and colleagues, 2012); "dramatically" and "replay at 20 times normal speed", which comes from rat studies, overstate it. |
| Guessing before you study (pre-testing) helps, even when the guess is wrong | **Good evidence** for the pretesting effect. The name *hypercorrection* belongs to a different finding: errors made with high confidence are the easiest to correct (Butterfield and Metcalfe, 2001). Any "burst of dopamine" is speculation. |
| Attention runs in 90-minute ultradian cycles, so plan 90-minute blocks | **Weak**: the cycle is an old hypothesis; a block of an hour or so with a break is sensible for other reasons, not this one. |
| Caffeine with L-theanine improves focus | **Small, short studies**. This library gives no doses; ask a doctor, and never about prescription stimulants without one. |

Only the first and third rows have lessons here; the others cannot be shown by a program without invented data.

Zakrajsek's chapter 4, "Improving the Learning Process", has one lesson here, [cognitive load](cognitive_load/README.md). The rest of it, and where the library already says why:

- **Four conditions for learning anything.** *Attention*: if you did not attend to it, for learning it never happened; a mind that wandered for twenty minutes of reading learned nothing from them. *Value*: a brain does not spend germane load on what you have told it you will never need, so find the use. *Understanding*: new material has to connect to a schema you already have. Vygotsky's *zone of proximal development* is the band between too easy (bored) and too hard (frustrated), and learning is fastest at its top edge; when you are over it, ask. *Anticipating success*: expecting to fail makes failing likelier, which [learned helplessness](learned_helplessness/README.md) turns into arithmetic.
- **Three memory processes**: *encoding* (taking it in), *consolidation* (stabilising it; sleep matters here, so cramming instead of sleeping costs twice), *retrieval* (getting it back out, which strengthens the trace again). A new trace is weak, and the next thing you learn can overwrite it (*interference*: you remember the second name at the party, not the first). Hence the book's advice to avoid back-to-back classes where you can, and to spend 30 minutes reviewing a class before the next one.
- **The forgetting curve**: about half of new material is gone in a day or two and 90% in a month. Zakrajsek's own Calculus 1 story shows the cost: high Bs and low As on four unit exams studied all night, then 37% on the final, because nothing had been reviewed. Cepeda and colleagues' review (2006) found spacing better in 259 of 271 comparisons. Study Tip 4.3: *review key material shortly after class, then once every few days.* That is [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md), with the arithmetic of why the reviews stay cheap.
- **Four ways to strengthen memory**: *elaboration* (connect it to what you know; rewrite class notes in your own words within 12 hours), *repetition* and the testing effect (each retrieval strengthens the trace: [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md)), *interleaving* (mix topics and problem types instead of studying in blocks; students rate it the least pleasant and it works better: [interleaving](interleaving/README.md) says why), and *spaced recall*.

## Beyond the lessons: motivation

McGuire's later chapters turn from strategy to motivation, and her Figure 8.1 sums them up in five things a student can do:

1. Use the learning strategies: the ones in these four lessons.
2. Cultivate a mindset that your intelligence can grow (Carol Dweck's *Mindset*).
3. Engage in positive, healthy self-talk.
4. Attribute positive and negative results to your behaviour, not to external circumstances. This is the move [metacognition](metacognition/README.md) asks for when a grade surprises you: in McGuire's words, a score "is not any indication of how smart you are. It's a reflection of your behaviors, the way you prepared for the first test."
5. Get adequate rest, nutrition and exercise. The brain is part of the body; persistent lack of sleep undermines health and grades, and people need different amounts, so find your own minimum and keep to it. The break in an [intense study session](spaced_retrieval/README.md#the-study-cycle-spacing-built-into-a-week) is the same idea on the scale of an hour.

Zakrajsek's chapter 5, "Exploring Social Influences", says the same about mindset, with practical steps: say "I can't *yet*" instead of "I can't"; work a little on something small in the area you "can't do", then a bit bigger, which is the start-small remedy of [learned helplessness](learned_helplessness/README.md); read failure as feedback (Study Tip 5.1: *seek out feedback whenever you can, and listen carefully to it*); and play against someone a little better than you. He is careful that results vary: a classmate who took AP Biology may need one hour for what takes you three, and you can still close the gap. His own example is writing, which he avoided for decades because he "couldn't write", until an editor persuaded him to try one chapter.

The same chapter names two biases that bend how we judge results. **Attribution bias**: we put our own failures down to circumstances and other people's to character. Study Tip 5.3: *if you don't do well on an exam, do not assume it was a poorly written test; that protects the ego and does not help you get better.* This is McGuire's point 4 above again. **Egocentric bias** (Greenwald, 1980): we remember our part in a success as larger, and in a failure as smaller, than it was, and our own opinions as more right than others', not from lying but because what *I* did is stored more deeply. Being self-aware reduces it, and [perspective taking](day_or_night/README.md) is the habit that does.

None of these is a lesson here, because none can be shown by a program without invented data; the [roadmap](../ROADMAP.md) says why, and the [resources](../RESOURCES.md) page lists the books.

## Learning with AI

McGuire's book was written in 2018, before a chatbot could explain any page on request. Almost nothing in this chapter changes because of that. What changes is which mistakes are easiest to make.

**What stays the same.** Memory is still built by what *you* do. McGuire's resident answered the drug question correctly from his phone and could not say why; "we can solve problems and do critical thinking only with information already stored in our brains." An AI is that phone, made fluent and patient. Every argument in the four lessons is about what happens in the learner's head, and none of them is about where the explanation came from.

**What gets worse.** Reading an AI's explanation is *rereading*, with every trap of [metacognition](metacognition/README.md): it is clear, confident and written for you, so it feels like knowing even more strongly than a textbook does. Asking it to solve the homework breaks strategy 11, doing homework without examples, in the most complete way possible, and it is always one click away. One field experiment in high-school mathematics (Bastani and colleagues, *PNAS*, 2025) gave students GPT-4 for practice problems. With plain access, practice scores rose, and on the exam taken without it those students scored about 17% lower than students who never had it. A version built to give hints instead of answers mostly avoided the loss.

**What gets better.** Everything this chapter says to do, an AI can make cheaper, if you ask it for the right thing:

| Instead of asking for | Ask for | Why, here |
|---|---|---|
| the answer | a hint, and only after you have tried | [metacognition](metacognition/README.md): produce before you look |
| an explanation to read | a quiz on it; write a confidence before each answer | [metacognition](metacognition/README.md): calibration |
| a summary | flashcards, then review them spaced | [spaced retrieval](spaced_retrieval/README.md) |
| more examples at the same level | questions at the level above: why, what if, find the flaw | [studying vs learning](studying_vs_learning/README.md) |
| a better explanation | to play a student while *you* explain, and ask the questions a class would | teach-the-material mode |
| the list of facts | the principle that produces them, then rebuild the list yourself | [count the vowels](count_the_vowels/README.md) |

A tutor built on these rules can do well: in a Harvard physics course (Kestin and colleagues, *Scientific Reports*, 2025), students working with an AI tutor designed around them learned more, in less time, than in an active-learning class.

**One new skill.** An AI can be wrong while sounding certain, so every answer it gives is a claim to check, and checking is Bloom's level 5, evaluating. This library has one rule for that, the same one it keeps for itself: a claim counts when a program that runs agrees with it. Ask the AI for the check, not just the claim, and run it.

**The rule of thumb:** use AI *after* you have tried, and use it to be *asked* questions more than to be *told* answers.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Ten rozdział nie jest o matematyce, tylko o tym, jak się jej uczyć. Opiera się na książce Saundry McGuire *Teach Yourself How to Learn*. Metakognicja to myślenie o własnym myśleniu, a jej najważniejszą, mierzalną częścią jest trafna ocena, ile naprawdę umiemy: kto tylko czyta notatki drugi raz, czuje się pewny i często się myli. Pamiętamy to, co robiliśmy z materiałem, a nie to, na co patrzyliśmy (ćwiczenie z liczeniem samogłosek). Taksonomia Blooma opisuje poziomy pracy z materiałem, od zapamiętania do tworzenia, a na najwyższym poziomie potrafimy odtworzyć zapomniane fakty. Wreszcie: powtórki rozłożone w czasie i sprawdzanie się z pamięci utrwalają wiedzę, a przy rosnących odstępach liczba powtórek rośnie tylko logarytmicznie. McGuire dodaje pięć sposobów na motywację: stosuj te strategie, wierz, że inteligencja może rosnąć, mów do siebie życzliwie, przypisuj wyniki swojemu zachowaniu, a nie okolicznościom, i dbaj o sen, jedzenie i ruch. A sztuczna inteligencja? Nie zmienia tego, jak działa pamięć: przeczytanie odpowiedzi od AI to ponowne czytanie, które daje złudzenie wiedzy. Używaj jej dopiero po własnej próbie i proś ją raczej o pytania, quizy i fiszki niż o gotowe odpowiedzi. Każdą jej odpowiedź sprawdzaj, najlepiej programem, który działa. Lekcja o dniu i nocy, według książki Todda Zakrajska *The New Science of Learning*, uczy patrzenia z perspektywy innych: gdy dwie osoby mówią „dzień” i „noc” o tym samym niebie, nie spierają się o fakty, tylko o próg, którego żadna nie nazwała. Ta sama książka opisuje samoregulację: wyznacz cel, zaplanuj drogę i trzymaj się planu. Najlepsza rada: zaczynaj od tak małych celów, jak trzeba, ale zawsze je wykonuj. Opisuje też poczucie własnej skuteczności (Bandura), czyli wiarę, że dasz radę. Rośnie ono z własnych sukcesów, z oglądania, jak robią to inni, z zachęty i z tego, jak odczytujemy własną tremę. Nie gwarantuje sukcesu, ale sprawia, że próbujemy, a dopiero próba daje szansę. Gdy po wielu porażkach przestajemy próbować, mówimy o wyuczonej bezradności; lekcja 6 pokazuje, że wychodzi się z niej małymi krokami, bo tylko próba daje nowe dowody. Rozdział 3 tej książki dotyczy planowania: cele SMART (konkretne, mierzalne, osiągalne, istotne, określone w czasie), cele ambitne z szansą około 10% i codzienna godzina rezerwy. Lekcja 7 pokazuje, dlaczego ta godzina jest potrzebna: zadania rzadko kończą się przed czasem, a często po nim, więc błędy szacunków się nie znoszą. Lekcja 8, o obciążeniu poznawczym, pokazuje, że pamięć robocza mieści około czterech porcji, a porcją jest to, co ćwiczenie uczyniło automatycznym; dlatego ten sam wzór to 19 symboli dla początkującego i jedna porcja dla eksperta. Lekcja 9 pokazuje, że arkusz zadań jednego typu pozwala pominąć wybór metody, a egzamin sprawdza głównie ten wybór; dlatego warto mieszać typy zadań. Rozdział 4 i 5 tej książki dodają: uwaga, wartość, zrozumienie i wiara w sukces to warunki nauki; pamięć to kodowanie, utrwalanie (także we śnie) i przypominanie; a zamiast „nie umiem” warto mówić „jeszcze nie umiem”. Całą drogę pamięci można narysować w jednej linii: uwaga i kodowanie, pamięć robocza (3–5 porcji), utrwalanie (sen), przechowywanie i przypominanie, które znów wzmacnia ślad. Popularne streszczenia wymieniają cztery techniki: przypominanie z pamięci, powtórki rozłożone w czasie, przeplatanie i łączenie nowego ze znanym. Pierwsze trzy mają tu lekcje i mocne dowody; twierdzenia o neuroprzekaźnikach, suplementach i 90-minutowych cyklach uwagi są znacznie słabiej potwierdzone. Syndrom oszusta to ten sam błąd w drugą stronę: ktoś, komu się udaje, wierzy, że zaraz zostanie zdemaskowany. Rada książki: słuchaj informacji zwrotnej i dowodów, a nie uczucia. Uczucie niewiedzy nie jest dowodem, tak jak uczucie wiedzy nie jest wiedzą.

## A note on the code

The programs are about people, so two of them rest on numbers that are not measurements, and they say so where they do. The learner in lesson 1 is invented, and the memory model in lesson 4 is a toy with the right shape. What is exact: the sun's height at dusk in lesson 5, the rule-of-succession arithmetic in lesson 6 (its learner is a model), the probabilities in lesson 7 (its task times are a model), the chunk counts in lesson 8 (its costs for a phone and a conversation are made up), the counts and exact volumes in lesson 9, the Brier score and why honesty minimises it, the vowel count and 15!, Euclid's formula checked against brute force, McGuire's published survey figures, and the SM-2 schedule. The books behind the chapter are listed on the [resources](../RESOURCES.md) page.
