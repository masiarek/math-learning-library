# Count the vowels: the task decides what you remember

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who has read a chapter and remembered none of it

**One line:** Memory keeps what you did with something, not what passed in front of your eyes: count the vowels in fifteen phrases and you remember about three of them; know that the goal is to remember, find the principle that orders them, and you remember twelve. Nobody got smarter in between.

## Do the exercise first

This is Saundra McGuire's exercise from chapter 3 of *Teach Yourself How to Learn* (Figure 3.3), and it only works if you do it before reading on. Set a timer for **45 seconds** and **count all the vowels** in these phrases:

| | |
|---|---|
| Dollar bill | Cat lives |
| Dice | Bowling pins |
| Tricycle | Football team |
| Four-leaf clover | Dozen eggs |
| Hand | Unlucky Friday |
| Six-pack | Valentine's Day |
| Seven-Up | Quarter hour |
| Octopus | |

Now scroll the table out of sight, and **write down as many of the phrases as you can remember.** Score yourself: the number you got, divided by 15, times 100.

In McGuire's workshops the average is **3 of 15, or 20%.** "Let's call it F minus."

Then she asks the group to look at the list again, reading each column from top to bottom, and find the principle that organises it. Try for fifteen seconds before you look at the program's output. Then give yourself another 45 seconds, this time to **memorise** the list, hide it, and write it down again. The workshop average for the second round is **12 of 15, or 80%**, and many people get all fifteen.

## What made the difference?

McGuire's question: "between our first and second attempts to recall the list, we had not become any smarter. So what made the difference?" Two things.

**The goal.** The first time, the task was to count vowels. Counting vowels needs the letters and never the meaning, so the letters are all your mind worked on, and what you did not work on, you did not keep. The second time you knew the goal was to remember. McGuire's warning is that students often do the first kind of task without knowing it: "read chapter 1" gets done as skimming chapter 1 between messages, and a problem set gets done to hand in correct answers, when the professor wanted the concepts that solve problems not on the sheet. A practice test gets memorised question by question, when it was a sample of what the concepts must be able to answer.

**The principle.** The list counts from 1 to 15: one dollar, two dice, three wheels, and on to a quarter of an hour. Once you see it, you no longer hold fifteen unrelated phrases. You hold one rule, and you walk through the numbers you already know, asking at each "what comes in n?". Section 4 of the program makes that concrete.

The second difference is also the fourth ability of [metacognition](../metacognition/README.md) at work: with the principle, a gap shows up by itself. If you reach 11 and find nothing, you know you forgot something and what to look for. Without it, fifteen phrases and twelve phrases feel much the same from the inside.

## Why this is a mathematics lesson

Section 3 is the reason mathematics is learnable at all. A random list has no shortcut, so it has to be stored item by item. A list with a principle can be regenerated from the principle, and the chance of fifteen items falling into order by accident, 1 in 15!, is about 1 in 1.3 trillion. Mathematics is almost nothing but principles of this kind. The [addition and multiplication rules for significant figures](../../01_Precision/significant_figures/README.md) are two rules to memorise until you see that one rule, about absolute and relative error, produces both. The [area and volume formulas](../../10_Geometry/area_and_volume_formulas/README.md) are eleven formulas until one check on their dimension tells them apart. Memorising the outputs of a principle is counting its vowels.

**5. A professor assigns "Read chapter 1". What goal does McGuire suggest the assignment really has?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

To understand the concepts of the chapter well enough to use them, not to have passed your eyes over every page. Reading while scrolling a phone is counting the vowels.

</details>

**6. Why is memorising the answers to a practice test a mistake?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The practice test is a sample of what the concepts must be able to do. The real test asks different questions, and only the concepts transfer to them.

</details>

**7. How does the principle let you check your own recall?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It says what a complete answer looks like: fifteen items, one for each number. A number with nothing next to it is a gap you can see and go looking for.

</details>

## How to practise

1. **Before you read or study, say the goal out loud.** Not "read chapter 1" but "be able to explain why the converse needs its own proof".
2. **Look for the principle before memorising the items.** When a page gives a list of rules, ask what produces all of them.
3. **Treat a practice test as a sample.** For each question, ask which concept it tests and make up a different question on the same concept.
4. **Try the exercise on someone else**, without telling them the goal the first time. It is the fastest way to believe the result.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

To ćwiczenie Saundry McGuire. Masz 45 sekund na policzenie samogłosek w piętnastu hasłach: banknot dolarowy, kostki do gry, trójkołowy rowerek, czterolistna koniczyna i tak dalej. Potem, bez uprzedzenia, masz wypisać z pamięci jak najwięcej haseł. Średnio ludzie pamiętają trzy z piętnastu, czyli 20%.

Za drugim razem wiedzą, że celem jest zapamiętanie, i szukają zasady porządkującej listę: hasła odpowiadają liczbom od 1 do 15 (jeden dolar, dwie kostki, trzy kółka, cztery listki, pięć palców… kwadrans to 15 minut). Średnio pamiętają dwanaście z piętnastu, czyli 80%. Nikt w tym czasie nie zmądrzał. Zmieniły się dwie rzeczy: znajomość celu i zasada, która porządkuje materiał.

Pamięć zatrzymuje to, co robiliśmy z materiałem, a nie to, co przesunęło się przed oczami. Liczenie samogłosek potrzebuje tylko liter, więc tylko litery zostają. W matematyce jest tak samo: zamiast uczyć się na pamięć wyników, trzeba znaleźć zasadę, z której wynikają, bo z niej da się je odtworzyć i sprawdzić, czy czegoś nie brakuje.
