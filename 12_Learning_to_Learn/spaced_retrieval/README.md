# Spaced retrieval: why the flashcards come back later

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who has used flashcards, or crammed the night before and lost it all a week later

**One line:** Memory fades fast and then slowly, and pulling a fact back out of memory, just as it starts to fade, strengthens it far more than reading it again; a schedule whose gaps grow by a fixed factor makes this cheap, because the number of reviews grows only with the logarithm of how long you want to remember.

## The idea

Two findings from memory research are behind every flashcard program, and behind the Anki decks on the pages of this library.

**Memory fades, fast at first.** Hermann Ebbinghaus measured it on himself in 1885, learning lists of nonsense syllables and testing how much he had kept: most of the loss comes in the first day, and what survives fades more and more slowly. A common model is R = e^(−t/S): the chance of recall R after t days, with S the memory's *stability*, the time it takes to fall to about 37%.

**Retrieval strengthens, and effortful retrieval strengthens most.** Answering a question from memory changes the memory: it lasts longer afterwards than it would have if you had read the answer again. This is the *testing effect*: in Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 experiment, students who tested themselves remembered more after a week than students who reread, while the rereaders felt surer (see [metacognition](../metacognition/README.md)). And a review does most when the fact has partly faded, so that getting it back takes effort. This is the *spacing effect*: the same number of reviews, spread out, beats the same number crammed together.

Cramming feels productive for the very reason it works badly. Every rereading an hour after the last succeeds, so it feels like progress, but the memory was never in danger and has little reason to get stronger.

## The arithmetic of spacing

If every review strengthens memory, you might expect to need a review every week forever. You do not, because each successful review makes the next gap longer. SuperMemo's SM-2 algorithm, published by Piotr Wozniak in 1987 and the starting point of Anki's scheduler, uses gaps of 1 day, 6 days, and then each gap 2.5 times the one before, rounded up, for as long as you keep answering right.

The review days then form a geometric series, so after k reviews the card has been kept for roughly 2.5^k days. Turning that round: **keeping a card for T days takes about log(T) / log(2.5) reviews.** Keeping it 2.5 times as long costs one more review. A fixed weekly review would cost T / 7. This is the same geometric growth as [compound interest](../../09_Calculus/velocity_equals_position/README.md), working for you.

## What the program prints

What the model does show honestly is the mechanism: under any rule where a review helps more the more has faded, four crammed reviews lose to four spaced ones. ## The study cycle: spacing built into a week

Saundra McGuire's *Teach Yourself How to Learn* (Figure 4.6, from Louisiana State University's Center for Academic Success, based on F. L. Christ's 1997 learning cycle) turns these findings into a routine of five steps:

1. **Preview** before class: skim the chapter, note headings and bold words, and come up with questions you would like the lecture to answer.
2. **Attend** class: go, answer and ask questions, take meaningful notes.
3. **Review** as soon as possible after class: read the notes, fill gaps, note questions. Explaining the class to yourself is a first retrieval, made a few hours after the class and not the night before the exam.
4. **Study** in intense study sessions: set specific goals (1–2 minutes), do active learning tasks with focus (30–50 minutes), take a break or have a reward (10–15 minutes), then review what you just studied (5 minutes). Sessions can be as short as 15–20 minutes or as long as 75–90, 50–60 being typical. Even a 10-minute one works: a minute of goals, eight of work, a minute of review, and some students do 10 to 20 of them a day. One student who turned his grades round credited "that Power Hour thing". Add a weekend review to connect the week's material, and questions like "why?", "how?" and "what if?".
5. **Assess**: ask whether your methods work and whether you understand the material well enough to teach it to others. If flashcards did not do it, try something else, such as concept maps.

Seen through this lesson, the cycle meets each idea several times at growing intervals: preview, class, review the same day, a study session within days, the weekend review, the exam. Each meeting is a retrieval rather than a rereading, if you make it one, and step 5 is the [metacognition](../metacognition/README.md) check.

## Questions

**1. In the model R = e^(−t/S) with S = 1 day, how much is left after one day? After three?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

e^(−1) ≈ 36.8% after one day, e^(−3) ≈ 5.0% after three.

</details>

**2. Why does a review an hour after the last one do so little?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The fact has hardly faded, so retrieving it takes no effort and the memory is barely strengthened. The spacing effect: reviews help most when some forgetting has happened.

</details>

**3. What is the testing effect?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Retrieving something from memory makes it last longer than reading it again for the same time.

</details>

**4. SM-2 gaps: 1, 6, then ×2.5 rounded up. List the first five gaps.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1, 6, 15, 38, 95 days.

</details>

**5. Why does keeping a card for ten years take only a few more reviews than keeping it for one?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The gaps grow geometrically, so the time covered grows like 2.5^k and the number of reviews like log T. Ten years is about 2.5^2.5 times one year, so it costs two or three more reviews (the program counts 6 for one year and 9 for ten).

</details>

**6. Name the five steps of McGuire's study cycle and the four parts of an intense study session.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Preview, attend, review, study, assess. Set a goal, study with focus, take a break or reward, review.

</details>

**7. The toy model's numbers are invented. What in its output can you still trust?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The direction, not the sizes: if a review helps more when more has been forgotten, cramming loses to spacing for the same number of reviews. The size of the effect has to come from experiments, not from this program.

</details>

## How to practise

1. **Answer before you look**, every time: a flashcard read front-then-back without an attempt is rereading.
2. **Review the same day, then let it fade a little.** After class, then in a few days, then at the weekend.
3. **Use the decks.** Every lesson here with a **Flashcards** section has an Anki deck; Anki does the spacing.
4. **Do not cram what you want to keep.** Cramming can get you through tomorrow's test; the program's day-30 column is what it costs.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Pamięć zanika szybko na początku, a potem coraz wolniej (krzywa zapominania Ebbinghausa). Dwa zjawiska pomagają ją utrwalić. Efekt testowania: przypomnienie sobie czegoś z pamięci wzmacnia ją bardziej niż ponowne przeczytanie. Efekt rozłożenia w czasie: powtórka działa najlepiej, gdy materiał już trochę wyblakł i przypomnienie wymaga wysiłku. Dlatego cztery powtórki w jeden wieczór dają mniej niż cztery powtórki rozłożone na tydzień.

Programy do fiszek, takie jak Anki, wydłużają odstępy między powtórkami: 1 dzień, 6 dni, a potem za każdym razem 2,5 raza dłużej. Odstępy tworzą ciąg geometryczny, więc liczba powtórek potrzebnych, żeby pamiętać coś przez T dni, rośnie jak logarytm z T. Rok to sześć powtórek, dziesięć lat to dziewięć. Przy powtórce co tydzień byłoby ich ponad pięćset.

Saundra McGuire proponuje „cykl nauki": przejrzyj materiał przed zajęciami, bądź na zajęciach, powtórz jeszcze tego samego dnia, ucz się w krótkich, intensywnych sesjach (cel, 30–50 minut skupienia, przerwa, 5 minut powtórki) i sprawdzaj, czy twoje metody działają. To jest właśnie rozłożone w czasie przypominanie, wbudowane w tydzień.
