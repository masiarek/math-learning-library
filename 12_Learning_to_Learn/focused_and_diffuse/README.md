# Focused and diffuse thinking: why trying harder can keep you stuck

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who has stared at one problem for an hour and got nowhere

**One line:** Focused thinking takes small steps from where your first idea landed, so it climbs the nearest hill and stops at its top, however long you keep at it; stepping back takes big steps, which find the right hill but not its top. You need both, one at a time: focus, step away, focus again.

## The picture

Barbara Oakley and Terrence Sejnowski's *Learning How to Learn*, written with Alistair McConville for children and teenagers (2018), gives its chapter 2 the title "Easy Does It: Why Trying Too Hard Can Sometimes Be Part of the Problem". It opens with a photo from 2004: thirteen-year-old Magnus Carlsen has got up from the board in the middle of a rapid game and wandered off to watch other games, while Garry Kasparov, then the best player in the world, sits thinking. The game was drawn. (Kasparov won their second game, and with it the match; the book tells only the first.) The book's lesson: *sometimes we need to lose concentration so we can think more clearly.*

The book says the brain works in two ways. In **focused mode** you pay attention to one thing: a math problem, a teacher, a puzzle. You need it first, to switch on the parts of the brain the problem needs. In **diffuse mode** the mind is relaxed and wanders: daydreaming, doodling, walking, showering. That is where new connections between ideas often come from. Learning goes back and forth between the two, and you can be in only one at a time.

The picture that explains it is a **pinball table**. In focused mode the rubber bumpers are close together, and the ball bounces around one small area, along trails laid by thoughts you have had before. In diffuse mode the bumpers are far apart, and the ball crosses the whole table, touching few bumpers on the way. Oakley used the same picture for adults in *A Mind for Numbers* (2014).

A pinball table is a search, and a search can be run. This page runs it.

## The table

The program makes a problem out of 60 positions in a row, each with a score, and the answer is the position with the highest score. Focused mode steps to a neighbour, one position left or right, whenever the neighbour scores higher: bumpers next to each other. Diffuse mode looks at every seventh position across the whole table: bumpers far apart. The first idea lands at position 10.

## Trying harder does not move you

Section 2 is the chapter's title in numbers. Focused mode climbs from S in two steps to a score of 60 and stops, because both neighbours are lower. Then the program lets it try harder, with 10, 100 and 1000 steps, and it ends at 60 every time. Being stuck here has nothing to do with effort. It comes from the size of the step: to reach the higher hill you first have to go *down*, and a focused climber never takes a step down.

This has a name in psychology: the **Einstellung effect** (German for "attitude" or "set"). In Abraham Luchins's water-jar experiments (1942), people who had solved several problems with one long method went on using it on problems that had a far shorter solution, and many failed a problem the long method could not solve, one that people who had not been primed solved easily. The first idea laid a trail, and the ball kept rolling down it. Oakley's books use the word, and the program shows the mechanism: a trail is a hill, and focus climbs whatever hill it starts on.

## Stepping back finds the hill, not the top

Section 3 is diffuse mode alone. Nine wide looks across the table find the best region, around position 35, far from anywhere focus would go. But the best look scores 80, not 97: bumpers seven apart skip over the peak. A daydream can suggest where the answer is, and it does not work out the details.

Section 4 puts them in the order the book gives: focus first, step back, focus again. Fifteen positions in all reach 97, which a thousand focused steps did not. The survey picks the hill; focus climbs it. And section 5 shows how much depended on luck when focus worked alone: it finds the answer from only 11 of the 60 starting points, the ones that already landed on the right hill.

## What the model leaves out

The program shows one half of the book's claim, the half about step size. It does not show the other half: that the diffuse mode works on what focus has loaded. Here the survey could run without any focused work first. In a head it cannot; a walk after an hour on a problem is different from a walk with no problem in mind, and the book insists the focused session comes first. Nor does the model say anything about brains. "Focused" and "diffuse" are the book's teaching words, not scientific terms; the nearest ideas in neuroscience are the brain's task-positive networks and its *default mode network*, active in rest and mind-wandering, and the match is loose.

What experiments do support is the effect itself. Psychologists call it **incubation**: setting a problem aside for a while and coming back to it. A review of 117 studies (Sio and Ormerod, 2009) found a real, moderate benefit, larger for problems with many possible answers, and larger when the break came after a long period of work on the problem, as the book says it should. The classic story is from mathematics. Henri Poincaré wrote that after weeks of failing on a problem about what he called Fuchsian functions, he set it aside for a geology trip, and the key idea came to him as he put his foot on the step of a bus, while talking about something else. He then checked it at his desk, at leisure: focus again.

## What the book says to do

- **Work first, then break.** A break is useful only when there is a problem loaded. The book's tool for this is the *Pomodoro*: 25 minutes of focus with the phone away, then a short reward that is truly different, such as a walk, music or a snack. That rhythm is focus, diffuse, focus, with a timer.
- **When stuck, change the step size, not the effort.** If ten minutes more on the same approach has changed nothing, section 2 says a hundred will not either.
- **On a test, hard start, then jump to easy.** The book's advice for exams: look at a hard problem first, and when you get stuck, move to an easy one and come back. The hard one keeps working in the background while you do the easy ones.
- **Start homework early.** Diffuse mode needs time between sessions, and a problem started the night before it is due never gets it. This is also why [spaced retrieval](../spaced_retrieval/README.md) spreads study out.
- **A break is not a phone.** Switching to messages or videos is new focused work, which fills the attention the diffuse mode was using.

## Try it

1. **Time your stuck.** Next time you are stuck, note the time. After 10 more minutes with no progress, stop, walk for 5, and write down whether the problem looks different when you come back.
2. **Name your first idea.** Before starting a problem, write your first approach down. When stuck, ask whether you are still on it: that is the hill you started on.
3. **Change the start.** Set `START` in the program to 30, then to 50. Where does focus end, and does the diffuse survey care?
4. **Change the bumpers.** Set `FAR` to 3, then to 15. Which survey finds the right hill, and how many looks does each cost?

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Barbara Oakley opisuje dwa tryby myślenia. W trybie skupionym (focused) pracujemy uważnie nad jednym zadaniem; w trybie rozproszonym (diffuse) umysł odpoczywa i błądzi: spacer, prysznic, rysowanie bez celu. Obrazem jest flipper: w trybie skupionym odbojniki stoją gęsto i kulka krąży po małym obszarze, po śladach, które już znamy; w rozproszonym stoją rzadko i kulka przelatuje przez cały stół. W danej chwili możemy być tylko w jednym trybie.

Program traktuje to jak szukanie najwyższego punktu na pagórkowatym terenie. Tryb skupiony robi małe kroki pod górę i zatrzymuje się na szczycie najbliższego wzgórza; tysiąc kroków kończy się tam, gdzie trzy, bo do wyższego wzgórza trzeba najpierw zejść w dół. Dlatego „bardziej się starać” często nie pomaga: problemem jest wielkość kroku, a nie wysiłek. Psychologia nazywa to efektem Einstellung. Tryb rozproszony robi duże skoki: znajduje właściwe wzgórze, ale nie jego szczyt. Dopiero oba naraz, jeden po drugim (skupienie, przerwa, znów skupienie), dają najlepszą odpowiedź.

Badania nad inkubacją (odłożeniem problemu na jakiś czas) potwierdzają, że przerwa po długiej pracy pomaga, choć nazwy „tryb skupiony” i „rozproszony” to metafora, a nie termin naukowy. Praktyczne rady z książki: najpierw pracuj, potem rób przerwę (technika Pomodoro: 25 minut skupienia i krótka nagroda); na teście zacznij od trudnego zadania, a gdy utkniesz, przejdź do łatwego i wróć; zaczynaj pracę domową wcześnie. Telefon to nie przerwa, tylko nowe zadanie dla uwagi.
