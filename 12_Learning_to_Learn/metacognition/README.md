# Metacognition: judging what you know

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who has handed in an exam sure it went well and got it back covered in red

**One line:** Of Flavell's four parts of metacognition, the last one, judging accurately how much you have learned, is a claim that can be tested: say how sure you are before you answer, and compare. A learner who does this sees their overconfidence in numbers, and the score that measures it rewards nothing but the truth.

## The idea

*Metacognition* is John Flavell's word (1976) for **thinking about your own thinking**. Saundra McGuire's *Teach Yourself How to Learn* (Figure 3.2) lists four aspects of his definition, the ability to:

1. think about your own thinking;
2. be consciously aware of yourself as a problem solver;
3. monitor, plan, and control your mental processing;
4. accurately judge your level of learning.

The first three are habits: asking "what exactly don't I get?", "which strategies do I have for this?", "is this working?". McGuire's version of the first is the question to ask while studying: **"Am I understanding this material, or just memorizing it?"**

The fourth is different, because it is a *claim*, and a claim can be checked. "I know this" is a prediction about what will happen when someone asks. This lesson is about checking that prediction, because it is the one that decides when you stop studying.

## Why the fourth ability fails

McGuire describes a scene most students know. You hand in an exam "beaming with pride, knowing that you've done a great job", and it comes back with a C, a D or an F. The obvious conclusion is "I'm not smart enough" or "I need to work harder". The accurate one is narrower: **your judgement of your own learning was wrong**, and it was wrong in a predictable direction.

The usual cause is rereading. Reading a page for the second time is easy, and the ease feels like knowing. But recognising something when you see it and producing it when you do not are different skills, and an exam asks for the second. In Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 experiment, students who reread a passage four times predicted they would remember more of it a week later than students who read it once and then tested themselves three times. A week later they remembered less.

The cure is not to try harder to feel the right amount of sure. It is to stop relying on the feeling: **answer before you look, and keep score.**

## Keeping score

Before each answer, write down a confidence: 50% (a guess between two options) up to 100% (certain). Afterwards mark it right or wrong. Two numbers then say how well you judge yourself:

- **The gap:** mean confidence minus the share you got right. Positive means overconfident.
- **The calibration table:** of the answers you called 90%, what share was right? A calibrated learner's 90% answers are right about 9 times in 10.

One more number combines them. The **Brier score** is the mean of (confidence − outcome)², with outcome 1 for right and 0 for wrong. Zero is perfect; saying 50% every time scores 0.25; saying 100% and being wrong costs a full 1.

Why this particular formula? Because it cannot be gamed. If you really get a kind of question right with probability p and you report q, the expected score is p(1 − q)² + (1 − p)q². Its derivative in q is 2(q − p), which is zero only at q = p. **The score is lowest when you report exactly what you believe.** Overclaiming does not pay, and neither does false modesty. A scoring rule with this property is called *proper*, and it is what turns "accurately judge your level of learning" from a matter of opinion into a measurement.

## Questions

**1. Which of Flavell's four abilities can you measure, and how?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The fourth, judging your level of learning. Write down a confidence before each answer, mark the answers, and compare confidence with results: the gap, the calibration table, or the Brier score.

</details>

**2. A student says 90% on ten questions and gets six right. Overconfident, underconfident, or calibrated? By how much?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Overconfident by 30 points: 90% claimed, 60% right.

</details>

**3. What is the Brier score of answering "100% sure" and being wrong? Of "50%" and being wrong?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1 − 0)² = 1, the worst possible. (0.5 − 0)² = 0.25.

</details>

**4. You believe you get a type of question right 70% of the time. Why not report 100%, to look confident?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because it raises your expected Brier score from 0.21 to 0.30. The expected score p(1 − q)² + (1 − p)q² is smallest at q = p, so the honest report is the best one.

</details>

**5. Rereading a chapter makes it feel familiar. Why is that feeling a poor guide?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Familiarity is recognition: the material feels known when it is in front of you. An exam asks for recall, producing the material when it is not in front of you. Only answering without looking tests recall.

</details>

**6. What is the question McGuire suggests asking while studying?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"Am I understanding this material, or just memorizing it?"

</details>

**7. A student's 100% answers are right 55% of the time and their 60% answers are right 55% of the time. What does that say about their confidence?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It says nothing about their answers. A useful confidence separates the answers that will be right from those that will not, and this one does not.

</details>

## How to practise

1. **Before every answer, write a number.** On homework, flashcards and the questions on these pages: 50, 60, 70, 80, 90 or 100.
2. **Keep the tally** for a week, then fill in a calibration table like the program's.
3. **Stop studying a topic on a test result, not on a feeling.** "I got 9 of 10 without looking" is a reason to move on. "It looks familiar" is not.
4. **When a grade surprises you, look at the gap before the grade.** The grade says how much you knew; the gap says how well you knew it, and you can fix that.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Metakognicja to według Johna Flavella „myślenie o własnym myśleniu". Saundra McGuire wymienia cztery jej elementy: myśleć o swoim myśleniu, być świadomym siebie jako kogoś, kto rozwiązuje problemy, kontrolować i planować własną pracę umysłową oraz trafnie oceniać, ile się naprawdę nauczyło.

Pierwsze trzy to nawyki. Czwarty to twierdzenie, a twierdzenie można sprawdzić: zanim odpowiesz na pytanie, zapisz, na ile procent jesteś pewien, a potem porównaj z wynikiem. Kto przeczytał notatki dwa razy, zwykle jest pewny na 90% i ma rację w połowie przypadków, bo znajome wydaje się znane. Rozpoznać coś, gdy się to widzi, a przypomnieć sobie bez podglądania, to dwie różne umiejętności, a egzamin sprawdza tę drugą.

Miarą jest wynik Briera: średnia z (pewność − wynik)². Ma on ważną cechę: najmniejszą wartość oczekiwaną daje podanie dokładnie tej pewności, którą się naprawdę ma. Przechwalanie się nie opłaca, fałszywa skromność też nie. Wniosek praktyczny: kończ naukę tematu po teście, a nie po uczuciu, że „już umiem".
