# Roots of unity

**Level:** 201 · for anyone who has read [multiplication rotates](../multiplication_rotates/README.md) and remembers that cos 30° = √3/2

**One line:** Multiplying by a unit point turns the plane by its angle, so multiplying by it n times turns n times as far, and a point at 1/n of a turn is back at (1, 0) after n steps: the equation zⁿ = 1 has exactly n solutions, spaced evenly around the circle, and the program finds them with no angle computed and no square root rounded.

## Where the picture pays off

The [previous lesson](../multiplication_rotates/README.md) showed that multiplying by a point of length 1 turns the plane by that point's angle and changes no lengths. It never computed an angle, and this page will not either. It asks the one question that "angles add" makes easy: **what happens when a unit point is multiplied by itself, over and over?**

It walks around the unit circle in equal steps. Each multiplication turns by the same angle A, so after n of them the point sits at angle nA. In cosines and sines,

```text
(cos A, sin A)ⁿ  =  (cos nA, sin nA)
```

which is **de Moivre's formula**, and it is "angles add" applied n − 1 times. Then the question splits. Most unit points walk forever and never land where they started. A few come back, exactly, after n steps: those are the **n-th roots of unity**, the solutions of zⁿ = 1, and there are exactly n of them.

## De Moivre's formula, exactly

Section 1 takes the 3-4-5 point z = (3/5, 4/5) and multiplies it by itself. Its angle A is whatever angle a 3-4-5 triangle has, and it is never computed. Every power has squared length 1, so the powers stay on the circle, and every coordinate is an exact fraction: z² = (−7/25, 24/25), z³ = (−117/125, 44/125).

That z² sits at angle 2A can be checked without knowing A, because the double-angle formulas say what (cos 2A, sin 2A) is in terms of cos A and sin A:

```text
cos 2A  =  2cos²A − 1     =  2 · 9/25 − 1        =  −7/25
sin 2A  =  2 sin A cos A  =  2 · (4/5) · (3/5)   =  24/25
```

Those are the coordinates of z². The triple-angle formulas, 4cos³A − 3cos A and 3 sin A − 4 sin³A, give (−117/125, 44/125), which is z³. The pair rule produced both by arithmetic. It does not know what an angle is, and it did not need to; the formulas are what "angles add" looks like written out.

Clear the denominators and every power is a Pythagorean triple: 7² + 24² = 25², 117² + 44² = 125², 527² + 336² = 625². A unit point with fractional coordinates squares to another one, and the two-squares identity of [lesson 1](../multiplication_as_pairs/README.md) is the reason.

## Most points never come back

Section 2 looks at those denominators. In lowest terms, the denominator of zⁿ is exactly 5ⁿ, for every n up to 24. A fraction with denominator 5ⁿ is not 1, so no power of the 3-4-5 point is (1, 0). Turning by A over and over never adds up to a whole number of turns, which says that A is not a fraction of a turn: not 1/12 of one, not 5/17 of one, not any p/q.

(The program checks 24 powers. That the pattern holds for all of them is a theorem: 5 never divides both numerators at once, which follows from how 5 factors among the integers a + bi. The page states it and does not prove it.)

So this is the typical unit point: it has fractional coordinates and its angle is an irrational number of turns. The points that do return are the exception, and they are what this page is about.

## Carrying √3 exactly

The unit point at 1/4 of a turn is (0, 1), and the previous lesson watched it come back after four steps. The one at 1/12 of a turn, 30°, is (cos 30°, sin 30°) = (√3/2, 1/2), and √3 is not a fraction. A float would round it, and then, as section 7 shows, twelve multiplications later the point is near (1, 0) and not at it. The word this page cares about is *exactly*, so the program does not round.

It keeps numbers of the form a + b√3 with a and b fractions, and multiplies them by

```text
(a + b√3)(c + d√3)  =  (ac + 3bd) + (ad + bc)√3
```

because √3 · √3 = 3. The product has the same form, so the arithmetic never leaves the set. Section 3 checks that (√3)² = 3, that (1 + √3)(1 − √3) = −2, and that (√3/2)² + (1/2)² = 1, so (√3/2, 1/2) is a unit point.

The pair rule from lesson 1 does not care what its coordinates are, so long as they can be added, subtracted and multiplied. The program's `mul` is the same four lines as before, and it handles fractions, fractions with √3 in them, and, in the last section, floats.

(That rule for a + b√3 is itself a rule on pairs, (a, b) · (c, d) = (ac + 3bd, ad + bc), and it is the complex rule with the −1 replaced by +3. The [roadmap](../../ROADMAP.md) has the siblings of the complex numbers waiting for a page; this is one of them, met early because it was needed.)

## The clock face

Section 4 raises h = (√3/2, 1/2) to every power from 0 to 12. It visits (√3/2, 1/2), (1/2, √3/2), (0, 1), (−1/2, √3/2), and so on around the circle to (√3/2, −1/2), and then h¹² = (1, 0): an equality of fractions, not a float near 1. Twelve steps of a twelfth of a turn is one turn.

The twelve points are the marks of a clock face, numbered counterclockwise from (1, 0). Among them are the four compass points the previous lesson reached with quarter turns, at marks 0, 3, 6 and 9, and the eight points with coordinates ±1/2 and ±√3/2, which are the cosines and sines of 30° and 60° and their reflections.

## zⁿ = 1 has exactly n solutions

An **n-th root of unity** is a solution of zⁿ = 1: a point that is back at (1, 0) after n multiplications by itself. Section 5 raises each of the twelve marks to the powers 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12 and records which results are (1, 0). Down the columns, z² = 1 has 2 solutions, z³ = 1 has 3, z⁴ = 1 has 4, z⁶ = 1 has 6, z¹² = 1 has 12. The solutions of zⁿ = 1 are the marks k that are multiples of 12/n, every fourth mark for the cube roots, every third for the fourth roots, so they are n points spaced evenly around the circle.

The table finds n solutions among the twelve marks. That they are *all* the solutions, anywhere in the plane, takes one more fact: a polynomial equation of degree n has at most n solutions in a field, and lesson 1 showed that the pairs are a field. So the marks are the only ones.

The last column is the first power at which each mark returns. Marks 1, 5, 7 and 11 need all twelve steps. They are the **primitive** twelfth roots of unity, and the powers of any one of them visit all twelve marks. Marks 2 and 10 return after six steps, so they are sixth roots of unity, and their powers visit only the even marks. In general mark k returns after 12 / gcd(k, 12) steps, and the primitive ones are the k with no factor in common with 12.

## The cube roots and the quadratic formula

Mark 4 is w = (−1/2, √3/2), and section 6 cubes it: w³ = (1, 0). The polynomial z³ − 1 factors as (z − 1)(z² + z + 1), so a cube root of unity other than 1 must solve z² + z + 1 = 0, and the program checks that w² + w + 1 = (0, 0), exactly.

The quadratic formula solves z² + z + 1 = 0 as well, and gives (−1 ± √−3)/2. School algebra stops there, at the square root of a negative number. In pairs, √−3 is the point (0, √3): the program squares it and gets (−3, 0), then computes (−1 + √−3)/2 as a pair and gets (−1/2, √3/2), which is w. The formula was right all along; it was waiting for somewhere to put √−3, and the place is the vertical axis.

The section ends with a fact that holds for every n > 1: **the n-th roots of unity add up to (0, 0)**. The three cube roots do, the four fourth roots do, all twelve marks do. Geometrically they are balanced around the origin. Algebraically the argument is two lines: if S is the sum and w is a primitive root, multiplying every root by w only shuffles the roots among themselves, so wS = S, so (w − 1)S = 0, and since w ≠ 1 and the pairs are a field, S = 0.

## The same thing in floats

Section 7 builds the same two points with `math.sqrt(3) / 2`, which is not √3/2 but the nearest double to it, about 10⁻¹⁶ away. Then w · w · w is not (1, 0), and h¹² is further off still. The pair rule was as exact as before; the coordinates were not, and the error in the starting point rode through every multiplication. [Chapter 1](../../01_Precision/README.md) is about what happens after that. This page avoided the problem by never rounding: the symbol √3 with the rule √3 · √3 = 3 is exact in a way no float can be.

The eighth roots of unity need √2 the same way. The fifth need √5 with another square root nested inside it, and cos(360°/n) is a fraction at all only for n = 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6, which is Niven's theorem. Twelve is the largest n whose roots of unity can be written with fractions and a single square root, which is why the page stopped at the clock.
