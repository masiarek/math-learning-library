# The derivative is a velocity

**Level:** 101 → 201 · for anyone who can multiply out (3 + h)², and who has watched a car's speedometer

**One line:** The velocity of a moving point at one instant is the number its average velocities settle on as the time interval shrinks; that number is the derivative, and knowing it is knowing how to take the next small step.

## Two arrows on a number line

The talk behind [Euler's identity](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md) draws e^t as an arrow on a number line labelled **Position**, and its derivative d/dt e^t as a second arrow starting at the tip of the first, labelled **Velocity**. Before e appears at all, there is the question of what the second arrow is. This page answers it with motions simple enough to compute exactly.

A point moves along a number line, and x(t) is where it is at time t. Between the times t and t + h it covers the distance x(t + h) − x(t), so its **average velocity** over that stretch is

```text
(x(t + h) − x(t)) / h          distance over time
```

A speedometer does not show an average, though. It shows the velocity *at an instant*, and an instant has no length: put h = 0 in the formula and it reads 0/0. The way out is to take shorter and shorter stretches and watch where the averages go.

## A steady motion

Section 1 moves a point by x(t) = 5 + 3t: it starts at 5 and gains 3 every unit of time. Every average velocity is 3, over a whole unit of time or over a thousandth of one. A steady motion has one velocity, and there is nothing to settle.

## A motion that speeds up

Section 2 moves the point by x(t) = t². Now the averages over [3, 3 + h] depend on h: 7 over a whole unit, 6.1 over a tenth, 6.01 over a hundredth. The program computes them as exact fractions, so the pattern is not approximate, and algebra says why:

```text
((3 + h)² − 9) / h  =  (6h + h²) / h  =  6 + h
```

The average is 6 + h exactly. As h shrinks the averages settle on 6, and no stretch of time gives 6 itself, because every stretch has some length. **The number the averages settle on is the velocity at the instant, and it is called the derivative.** At the instant t = 3 the velocity of t² is 6; the same algebra at any t gives 2t + h, which settles on 2t, and section 2 prints it for t = 0 to 4. The derivative of t² is 2t.

"Settle on" is what a calculus course calls a *limit*, and it makes the phrase precise. For following the talk the picture is enough: the velocity is what the average velocities over shorter and shorter times are heading for.

## The notation

Section 3 names it. d/dt x, x′(t) and dx/dt are three spellings of the same thing, the velocity of x at time t. The d is for *difference*: dx is a small change in position, dt a small change in time, and dx/dt their ratio when both are very small. So d/dt (5 + 3t) = 3 and d/dt t² = 2t. On the talk's slides, d/dt e^t sits in a box labelled Velocity, and this is all the box means.

## Stepping with a velocity

The reason to know a velocity is that it says where the point goes next. Over a short time dt the position moves by about velocity · dt:

```text
new position  ≈  position + velocity · dt
```

That is the talk's picture exactly: the velocity arrow starts at the tip of the position arrow, and a short time later the position has moved a little way along it. Section 4 uses the rule to rebuild x = t² from its velocity 2t alone, starting at 0 and stepping to t = 3. Three steps reach 6, thirty reach 8.7, three thousand reach 8.997, and the true position is 9. Each step uses the velocity at its start, which is a little too low for a motion that is speeding up, and the shortfall is exactly 9/n, so smaller steps give smaller errors.

The same stepping is how the next lesson finds e, and how [Euler's identity](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md#the-talks-version-velocity-is-position-turned-a-quarter-turn) walks a point round a circle. It has a name, Euler's method, after the same Euler.

## In floats, the step cannot shrink forever

Exactly, the average for t² at t = 3 is off by h, so a smaller h is always better. Section 5 does the same computation in floating point and finds the opposite past a certain point: the error falls to about 4 × 10⁻⁸ at h = 10⁻⁸ and then climbs, until at h = 10⁻¹⁵ the answer is 5.33. (3 + h)² and 9 agree in more and more leading digits, and subtracting them leaves only rounding. That is [catastrophic cancellation](../../01_Precision/catastrophic_cancellation/README.md), and it means a program cannot find a derivative by making h tiny; the best it can do this way is about half the digits of a double. The lessons in this chapter measure velocities with h = 10⁻⁶, near the sweet spot, and print six decimals.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Punkt porusza się po osi liczbowej, a x(t) to jego położenie w chwili t. Średnia prędkość między t a t + h to przebyta droga przez czas: (x(t + h) − x(t)) / h. Prędkość w jednej chwili, tę ze szybkościomierza, dostaje się, skracając odcinek czasu i patrząc, do czego dążą średnie. Dla x = t² w chwili 3 średnia to dokładnie 6 + h, więc dąży do 6, choć żaden odcinek czasu nie daje samego 6. Ta liczba to pochodna, zapisywana d/dt x, x′(t) albo dx/dt; pochodna t² to 2t.

Prędkość mówi, gdzie punkt będzie za chwilę: nowe położenie ≈ położenie + prędkość · dt. To dokładnie obrazek z wykładu, strzałka prędkości zaczepiona na końcu strzałki położenia, i to z niego następna lekcja buduje e^t. Pułapka jest w komputerze: w liczbach zmiennoprzecinkowych h nie może maleć bez końca, bo odejmowanie prawie równych liczb zjada cyfry, i najlepsze h to około 10⁻⁸.
