# Radians

**Level:** 101 → 201 · for anyone who knows what an angle is, and has wondered why calculus stops using degrees

**One line:** An angle in radians is the length of the arc it cuts from the circle of radius 1, so a point walking round that circle at speed 1 is at angle t after time t; that is why d/dt sin t = cos t holds only in radians, and why e^{iπ} = −1 has a π in it and not 180.

## The talk's half circle

One of the talk's first pictures is a circle of radius 1 with the upper half of it drawn in red and labelled **π**, next to e^{πi} = −1. The label is a length. Half of that circle is π long, and the picture says: walk π along the circle from the point 1, and you arrive at −1. Nothing about degrees appears, and this page is about why.

## How long is the circle?

Section 1 measures it without using π, the way Archimedes did around 250 BC. A regular hexagon inside the circle of radius 1 has sides of length 1, so its perimeter is 6. Doubling the number of sides pushes the polygon out toward the circle: 12 sides give 6.2117, 24 give 6.2653, 96 give 6.2821, which is as far as Archimedes went, and 6144 give 6.283185. The perimeters settle on 2π = 6.283185…, so **the whole circle is 2π long, half of it π, and a quarter of it π/2.** (Each doubling uses the new side s′ = s / √(2 + √(4 − s²)), the form that avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers; the textbook form does subtract them, and loses its digits the way [catastrophic cancellation](../../01_Precision/catastrophic_cancellation/README.md) describes.)

## A radian is one radius of arc

Degrees cut the circle into 360 parts because the Babylonians counted in sixties. Radians measure an angle by the arc it cuts: the angle whose arc is as long as the radius is **one radian**, and an angle of t radians cuts an arc of length t from the unit circle. Section 2 lines the two units up. A quarter turn is 90° and π/2 radians, a half turn is 180° and π, a whole turn is 360° and 2π, and one radian is 57.2958°, a little under 60. It is not a round number of degrees because 2π is not a round number.

## The angle is the distance walked

Section 3 checks the definition by walking. From (1, 0) to the point at angle t it takes n straight steps along the circle and adds up their lengths. With one step the walk is a single chord, too short; with a thousand it is 1.000000 for t = 1, 1.570796 for t = π/2 and 3.141591 for t = π. The walk settles on t itself: in radians, **the point at angle t has walked a distance t**. That sentence is the only thing radians add, and everything else on this page follows from it.

## The velocity of a turning point

Now let a point turn: at time t it is at angle t, position (cos t, sin t). Section 4 measures its velocity the way [the derivative is a velocity](../derivative_as_velocity/README.md) does, and gets (−sin t, cos t) at every t. That is the position [turned a quarter turn](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md#multiplying-by-0-1-is-a-quarter-turn), at right angles to it (the dot product is 0) and of length 1. Read one coordinate at a time, it is the two derivatives every calculus book lists:

```text
d/dt cos t  =  −sin t          d/dt sin t  =  cos t          t in radians
```

They hold because the point walks a distance t in time t, so its speed is 1. Measure the same turning in degrees, with the point at angle d degrees at time d, and it walks only π/180 of a unit per degree: section 4 measures a speed of 0.017453, which is π/180, and in degrees every derivative of sin and cos carries that factor. Radians are the unit that makes it 1.

Section 5 shows the same thing in one line: for a small angle x, sin x / x is almost exactly 1 in radians, because a small arc is almost straight and its height is almost its length; in degrees the ratio is π/180 = 0.017453 however small x gets.

This is the step [Euler's identity](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md#the-talks-version-velocity-is-position-turned-a-quarter-turn) needs. The motion e^{it} has velocity i · e^{it}, the position turned a quarter turn, of length 1: it is this page's turning point. After a time t it has walked t round the circle, so it is at angle t radians, (cos t, sin t); and after a time π it has walked half the circle, to −1. In degrees the identity would read e^{i·180°} = −1 with a conversion factor hidden inside the e, which is why nobody writes it that way.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Kąt w radianach to długość łuku, który ten kąt wycina z okręgu o promieniu 1. Program mierzy okrąg wielokątami Archimedesa, bez użycia π: sześciokąt ma obwód 6, a każde podwojenie liczby boków przybliża obwód do 2π = 6,283185. Pół okręgu ma długość π, ćwierć π/2, a jeden radian to kąt, którego łuk jest równy promieniowi, około 57,3°.

Stąd jedyne, co radiany wnoszą: punkt pod kątem t przeszedł po okręgu drogę t. Jeśli punkt w chwili t jest pod kątem t, to porusza się z prędkością 1, a jego prędkość (−sin t, cos t) to położenie obrócone o 90°. Dlatego pochodna sin t to cos t tylko w radianach; w stopniach każda pochodna ma dodatkowy czynnik π/180. I dlatego w tożsamości Eulera jest π, a nie 180: ruch e^{it} to właśnie taki obracający się punkt, a po czasie π przeszedł pół okręgu i jest w −1.

## Auf Deutsch: Stichwörter

Im Bogenmaß ist der Winkel die auf dem Einheitskreis zurückgelegte Strecke, und nur dann gilt (sin t)′ = cos t.

**Stichwörter:** Bogenmaß (Radiant), Gradmaß, Einheitskreis, Bogenlänge, Umfang 2π, Ableitung des Sinus, Umrechnung.
