# Velocity equals position

**Level:** 201 · for anyone who has read [the derivative is a velocity](../derivative_as_velocity/README.md)

**One line:** Start a point at 1 and let it always move with velocity equal to its position: where it is at time t is e^t, e is where it is at time 1, and the law e^(a+b) = eᵃ · eᵇ, the talk's "double" and its "flip and squish" all come out of that one rule.

## The talk's first rule

The talk writes one equation in a box and draws it:

```text
d/dt e^t  =  e^t          e⁰ = 1
velocity     position
```

In words: a point starts at 1, and at every moment its velocity is equal to its position. At 1 it moves at speed 1, at 2 at speed 2, at 10 at speed 10, so it runs away faster the farther it gets. That rule, and the starting point, are the whole definition of e^t. No base, no exponent, no multiplying e by itself: e^t is *where the point is at time t*, and the number e is where it is at time 1.

## e is where the motion is at time 1

Section 1 moves the point by [stepping with its velocity](../derivative_as_velocity/README.md#stepping-with-a-velocity). Over a short time dt it moves by velocity · dt, which is position · dt, so each step multiplies the position by (1 + dt). Steps of 1/n from time 0 to time 1 multiply 1 by (1 + 1/n), n times: 2, 9/4, 64/27, 625/256, and then, in floats, 2.5937, 2.7048, 2.7169 and 2.71827, heading for e = 2.71828. The steps are exactly **compound interest**, (1 + 1/n)ⁿ, which is how [Euler's identity](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md#what-does-it-mean) and the [precalculus reading guide's](../../reading_guides/precalculus/README.md#what-a-precalculus-problem-looks-like) money example meet e. Money that earns interest at a rate equal to itself is a point whose velocity is its position.

Section 2 steps to other times: e^0.5 = 1.6487, e² = 7.389, and for t = −1 the steps run backwards to 1/e. At t = 0 the point has not moved, which is why e⁰ = 1 needs no argument here. Section 3 measures the velocity of `math.exp` directly, as an average over a millionth of a unit of time, and finds it equal to the position to six places at t = 0, 1, 2 and 3.

## The law of exponents, from the motion

Every step multiplies the position by the same factor (1 + dt), wherever the point is. So a point started twice as far out is twice as far out at every later time: the motion scales. Section 4 turns that into the law of exponents:

> Run the motion from 1 for a time a, and the point is at eᵃ. The rest of its motion is the motion from 1, scaled by eᵃ, so after a further time b it is at eᵃ · eᵇ. But it has run for a + b in all, so it is at e^(a+b). Therefore e^(a+b) = eᵃ · eᵇ.

The program checks it exactly on steps of 1/10, where eight steps from 1 and three steps followed by five more both give 1.1⁸ = 214358881/100000000, and with `math.exp`, where e^0.3 · e^0.5 and e^0.8 agree. **Adding times multiplies positions.** That is the law [maps that keep the laws](../../06_Algebraic_Structures/maps_that_keep_the_laws/README.md) found in the logarithm, run the other way, and it is the only law of exponents [Euler's identity](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md) needs. It is also why the motion, unlike "e multiplied by itself t times", makes sense for any t at all.

## Double, or flip and squish

The talk's next slides change the rule: velocity = 2 · position, labelled **Double**, then velocity = −0.5 · position, labelled **Flip and squish**. The solution of velocity = k · position starting at 1 is e^(kt), and the reason is short: it is the same motion with the clock running k times as fast. At time t the fast clock reads kt, so the point is at e^(kt); and a clock k times as fast makes every velocity k times as large, so

```text
d/dt e^(kt)  =  k · e^(kt)
```

That factor k is the **chain rule**, in the one case the talk needs: speeding up the clock by k multiplies every velocity by k. Section 5 measures it. For k = 2 the velocity is twice the position, at t = 0.29 where the talk stops at e^(2·0.29) = 1.786. For k = −0.5 the velocity points back toward 0 and is half as long as the position, so the point creeps toward 0 and never reaches it: e^(−0.5·0.60) = 0.741, the talk's 0.74….

The talk's last case is k = i: velocity = i · position, the position **turned 90°**. A velocity at right angles to the position cannot move the point toward 0 or away from it, only round it, and that is [Euler's identity](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md#the-talks-version-velocity-is-position-turned-a-quarter-turn). It needs [multiplication rotates](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md) to say what "i times an arrow" means, and [radians](../radians/README.md) to say how far round the point has gone.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Punkt startuje z 1 i w każdej chwili porusza się z prędkością równą swojemu położeniu: w 1 z prędkością 1, w 2 z prędkością 2. To jest cała definicja e^t: e^t to miejsce, w którym punkt jest w chwili t, a liczba e to miejsce w chwili 1. Nie trzeba mnożyć e przez siebie t razy. Mały krok ruchu mnoży położenie przez (1 + dt), więc kroki co 1/n dają (1 + 1/n)ⁿ, procent składany, który dąży do e = 2,71828.

Każdy krok mnoży przez ten sam czynnik bez względu na to, gdzie jest punkt, więc ruch zaczęty dwa razy dalej jest w każdej chwili dwa razy dalej. Stąd prawo potęg: po czasie a punkt jest w eᵃ, reszta ruchu to ruch z 1 przeskalowany przez eᵃ, więc po dalszym czasie b jest w eᵃ · eᵇ, a razem minęło a + b. Prędkość k razy położenie to ten sam ruch na zegarze chodzącym k razy szybciej: e^(kt), a pochodna ma czynnik k (reguła łańcuchowa). k = 2 podwaja, k = −0,5 odwraca i ściska, a k = i obraca o 90° i prowadzi po okręgu, do tożsamości Eulera.
