# Power series

**Level:** 201 · for anyone who has read [velocity equals position](../velocity_equals_position/README.md)

**One line:** If e^x is a polynomial that never ends, the rule "velocity = position" forces every one of its coefficients, 1/k!; the sums reach e in a dozen terms, and with an imaginary input the terms split into the series for cos and sin, which is Euler's formula read off a list of fractions.

## The talk's other road

The talk takes a second road to e^{πi} = −1. It writes

```text
e^x  =  1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 + x⁴/24 + ⋯ + xⁿ/n! + ⋯
```

puts x = πi in, and draws the terms as arrows laid end to end, each one turning a quarter turn from the one before, spiralling in onto −1. That picture is section 8 of [Euler's identity](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md#the-series-the-way-a-course-proves-it). This page is about the line above it: where the series comes from, and why its coefficients are 1, 1, 1/2, 1/6, 1/24.

## The coefficients are forced

Suppose e^x can be written as a polynomial that never ends, a₀ + a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³ + ⋯. The derivative of x^k is k x^(k−1) (the case k = 2 is [d/dt t² = 2t](../derivative_as_velocity/README.md#a-motion-that-speeds-up)), so differentiating term by term gives a₁ + 2a₂x + 3a₃x² + ⋯. The rule from [velocity equals position](../velocity_equals_position/README.md) says the two lists are the same function, so they match coefficient by coefficient:

```text
a₁ = a₀        2a₂ = a₁        3a₃ = a₂        …        k·aₖ = aₖ₋₁
```

With e⁰ = 1, a₀ = 1, and then there is no choice left: a₁ = 1, a₂ = 1/2, a₃ = 1/6, a₄ = 1/24, and aₖ = 1/k!. Section 1 builds them as exact fractions from the rule alone. Section 2 checks the reason: differentiate the sum up to x⁶ and the result is exactly the sum up to x⁵. **Each term's derivative is the term before it**, so the sum that never stops is its own derivative.

## At x = 1 the sums reach e, fast

Section 3 adds the terms at x = 1: 1, 2, 5/2, 8/3, 65/24, and by thirteen terms 2.7182818283, nine correct decimals. Compounding, [(1 + 1/n)ⁿ](../velocity_equals_position/README.md#e-is-where-the-motion-is-at-time-1), needed a hundred thousand steps for four. The terms 1/k! shrink faster than any power of 10, which is why a calculator computes e^x from a series and not by compounding.

Section 4 tries other x. Each term is the one before it times x/k, so the terms grow while k is less than x and shrink after: at x = 10 the largest is 10⁹/9! ≈ 2756, and forty terms still land on e¹⁰ = 22026.47. That growing-then-shrinking is the talk's spiral: at x = πi each arrow is the one before it times πi/k, a quarter turn and a scaling by π/k, so the arrows grow up to k = 3 and then shrink ever faster.

## Put in it: cos and sin fall out

Section 5 puts an imaginary number in. The powers of i cycle through 1, i, −1, −i, which is the [quarter turn](../../03_Complex_Numbers/multiplication_rotates/README.md#multiplying-by-0-1-is-a-quarter-turn) four times round, so the even terms of e^{it} are real with alternating signs and the odd terms are imaginary with alternating signs:

```text
real part        1 − t²/2 + t⁴/24 − t⁶/720 + ⋯          = cos t
imaginary part   t − t³/6 + t⁵/120 − t⁷/5040 + ⋯        = sin t
```

Summed with thirty terms they agree with `math.cos` and `math.sin` to six places at t = 1, π/2, π and 2. So e^{it} = cos t + i sin t, [Euler's formula](../../03_Complex_Numbers/eulers_identity/README.md), as a fact about coefficients. Section 6 differentiates the two lists term by term and finds d/dt sin t = cos t and d/dt cos t = −sin t, the facts [radians](../radians/README.md) measured on a turning point: the series speak radians, because the t in them is the arc length.

## What this page does not prove

It assumed that e^x *can* be written as a series, and it checked, rather than proved, that the series settles on a number for every x and that the number is the e^x of the motion. Those are theorems, the subject of a chapter called "Taylor series" or "Power series" in a second calculus course, and the books in [where this is taught](../README.md#where-this-is-taught) prove them. What this page shows is why the coefficients could not have been anything else.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Załóżmy, że eˣ da się zapisać jako wielomian, który się nie kończy: a₀ + a₁x + a₂x² + ⋯. Pochodna liczona wyraz po wyrazie to a₁ + 2a₂x + 3a₃x² + ⋯, a reguła „prędkość = położenie" każe obu listom być równymi, więc k·aₖ = aₖ₋₁. Z e⁰ = 1 wynika a₀ = 1 i dalej nie ma już wyboru: aₖ = 1/k!. Pochodna każdego wyrazu to wyraz poprzedni, więc suma bez końca jest swoją własną pochodną. Trzynaście wyrazów daje e z dziewięcioma poprawnymi cyframi po przecinku; procent składany potrzebował stu tysięcy kroków na cztery.

Po wstawieniu it potęgi i krążą: 1, i, −1, −i, więc wyrazy parzyste są rzeczywiste, a nieparzyste urojone. Część rzeczywista to szereg cos t, a urojona to szereg sin t, i stąd e^{it} = cos t + i sin t, wzór Eulera odczytany ze współczynników. Na wykładzie te wyrazy to strzałki, każda obrócona o 90° względem poprzedniej, które najpierw rosną, a potem maleją i zwijają się w spiralę kończącą się w −1.
