# Area and volume formulas

**Level:** 101 · for anyone reviewing geometry before precalculus, or anyone who mixes up 4πr² and (4/3)πr³

**One line:** A formula's power of length is its dimension: scale every length by k and a perimeter grows by k, an area by k² and a volume by k³, so a misremembered formula can be caught before a single number goes in.

## The idea

The book's second objective is a box of formulas to know: rectangle, triangle, circle, box, sphere, cylinder. Memorising eleven formulas is hard; memorising one check that tells them apart is easy. Every formula in the box multiplies lengths together:

- a **perimeter** or **circumference** is a length: no two lengths multiplied, 2l + 2w, 2πr;
- an **area** or **surface area** multiplies two lengths: lw, ½bh, πr², 4πr²;
- a **volume** multiplies three: lwh, πr²h, (4/3)πr³.

So count the lengths in each term. One is a length, measured in feet; two is an area, in square feet; three is a volume, in cubic feet. The constants in front, 2, ½, π, 4/3, are pure numbers with no units, and they do not count. If you double every length of a shape, its perimeter doubles, its area grows 2² = 4 times, and its volume 2³ = 8 times. A "volume" that only quadruples is really an area.

This page follows Section A.2, *Geometry Essentials*, objective 2, in Michael Sullivan's *Precalculus*: Example 4 and problems 2, 3, 6, 9 and 27–51.

## The formulas

| Shape | Measures | Formula | Lengths multiplied | Units |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| rectangle, length l, width w | perimeter | P = 2l + 2w | 1 | ft |
| | area | A = lw | 2 | ft² |
| triangle, base b, altitude h | area | A = ½bh | 2 | ft² |
| circle, radius r, diameter d = 2r | circumference | C = 2πr = πd | 1 | ft |
| | area | A = πr² | 2 | ft² |
| closed box, l × w × h | surface area | S = 2lh + 2wh + 2lw | 2 | ft² |
| | volume | V = lwh | 3 | ft³ |
| sphere, radius r | surface area | S = 4πr² | 2 | ft² |
| | volume | V = (4/3)πr³ | 3 | ft³ |
| closed right circular cylinder, radius r, height h | surface area | S = 2πr² + 2πrh | 2 | ft² |
| | volume | V = πr²h | 3 | ft³ |

Three things in this table catch people out:

- **The altitude of a triangle is not a side.** It is the height measured *at a right angle* to the base, from the base to the opposite corner. For a slanted triangle it is shorter than the slanted sides, and it may even fall outside the triangle.
- **Radius or diameter.** The circle formulas use the radius. When a problem gives the diameter, halve it first, or use C = πd.
- **"Closed".** A closed box or cylinder has a top and a bottom, and the surface area counts them. For an open can, drop one πr².

## Where the formulas come from

None of these is arbitrary. A rectangle l by w holds l · w unit squares. A triangle is half of a rectangle with the same base and height, so ½bh. A box l × w × h holds lwh unit cubes, and its surface is six rectangles in three matching pairs. A cylinder is a circle πr² stacked to height h, so its volume is πr²h, and its side, unrolled, is a rectangle 2πr wide and h tall, so its surface is the two circles plus 2πrh.

The sphere is the famous one. Archimedes proved that a sphere's surface equals the side of the cylinder that just fits around it: radius r, height 2r, side area 2πr · 2r = 4πr². And the volume and the surface are tied together: coat a sphere in a skin of thickness t, and the skin's volume is about the surface area times t. So the surface is the rate at which the volume grows as the radius grows, and 4πr² is what (4/3)πr³ gains per unit of radius. Section 3 of the program shows this. It is the same for a circle: a thin ring around it has area about 2πr · t, the circumference times the width. (In calculus words, each formula is the derivative of the next one up.)

Section 4's problem 32 is worth a second look. At r = 2 the area is 4π and the circumference is 4π, the *same number*. That is a coincidence of units: one is 4π square feet and the other 4π feet, and they can no more be equal than 4 pounds can equal 4 miles. Change the unit to inches and the two numbers part company.

## Questions

Try each one before opening the answer.

**1. Fill in (problems 2 and 3): the area of a triangle with base b and altitude h is ___; the circumference of a circle of radius r is ___.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A = ½bh and C = 2πr.

</details>

**2. Problem 6: which is the volume of a sphere of radius r: (a) (4/3)πr², (b) (4/3)πr³, (c) 4πr³, (d) 4πr²? Rule out as many as you can without remembering.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b). The dimension test rules out (a) and (d): r² makes an area. Between (b) and (c), the sphere fits inside a cube of side 2r, whose volume is 8r³, and 4π ≈ 12.6 is more than 8, so (c) is too big. (4/3)π ≈ 4.19, about half the cube, which is believable.

</details>

**3. True or false (problem 9): the surface area of a sphere of radius r is (4/3)πr².**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

False; it is 4πr². This one the dimension test cannot catch, because (4/3)πr² *is* an area in shape. Only the constant is wrong, which is why it is a favourite trick question.

</details>

**4. Every side of a box is tripled. What happens to its volume and to its surface area?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The volume grows 3³ = 27 times and the surface area 3² = 9 times. This is why big animals have thick legs and small ones lose heat fast: weight follows volume, strength and skin follow area.

</details>

**5. A pizza of diameter 16 inches costs twice as much as one of diameter 10 inches. Which is the better deal?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Area goes with the square of the diameter: (16/10)² = 2.56. The big one has 2.56 times the pizza for 2 times the price, so the big one wins. Diameter alone suggests only 1.6 times as much, which is the common mistake.

</details>

**6. Problems 37 and 38 swap the radius and the height of a cylinder (9 and 8). Do the volumes match? Why not?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No: π·9²·8 = 648π against π·8²·9 = 576π. The radius is squared and the height is not, so the radius counts for more.

</details>

**7. Problem 41: a square of side 2 is inscribed in a circle. Find the area of the circle.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The diagonal of the square is a diameter. By Pythagoras, the diagonal is √(2² + 2²) = √8, so r = √8/2 and r² = 8/4 = 2. Area πr² = 2π ≈ 6.28. Note that r² was needed, not r, so √8 never had to be simplified.

</details>

**8. Problem 47: how far does a wheel of diameter 16 inches travel in four revolutions?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

One turn rolls out one circumference, πd = 16π inches. Four turns: 64π ≈ 201.06 inches, about 16.8 feet. The trap is 2πd: the 2 in 2πr is there because r is half of d.

</details>

**9. Problem 49: a square of side 6 ft with a border 2 ft wide all round. Find the area of the border.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The outer square has side 6 + 2 + 2 = 10, not 6 + 2 = 8: the border is on both sides. 100 − 36 = 64 ft².

</details>

**10. A circle's area and its circumference are the same number. What is its radius, and why is the question a little dishonest?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

πr² = 2πr gives r = 2. It is dishonest because the two have different units, square units and units, so "equal" depends on the unit chosen. In inches instead of feet the numbers would differ.

</details>

**11. A triangle has base 10 and slanted sides 13 and 13. Why is its area not ½ · 10 · 13?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

13 is a side, not the altitude. The altitude drops from the top to the middle of the base, making two right triangles with hypotenuse 13 and leg 5, so the altitude is √(169 − 25) = 12, and the area is ½ · 10 · 12 = 60.

</details>

**12. Why is the surface area of a sphere "what the volume gains per unit of radius"?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Growing the radius by a small t adds a thin skin, and the skin is roughly the surface spread to thickness t: volume ≈ 4πr² · t. So the gain per unit of t tends to 4πr². The program's section 3 watches the ratio settle as t shrinks.

</details>

## How to practise

1. **The dimension check first, every time.** Before using a formula, count the lengths multiplied in each term and say the unit out loud: "cubic feet". A wrong unit means a wrong formula.
2. **Problems 27–51 on paper, then compare with the output**: exact answers first (25π), decimals last.
3. **Sketch before you compute.** The shaded-region problems 39–42 and 49–51 are always a sum or a difference of shapes that have formulas; say which before writing numbers.
4. **The cards**, a few minutes a day. The `trap` cards are the ones worth failing on purpose.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Wzorów na pola i objętości jest jedenaście, ale wystarczy jedna kontrola, żeby ich nie mylić: policz, ile długości mnoży się w każdym wyrazie. Obwód to jedna długość (2πr), pole dwie (πr², 4πr²), objętość trzy (πr²h, (4/3)πr³). Stałe z przodu, takie jak 2, ½, π, 4/3, to czyste liczby i się nie liczą. Jeśli podwoimy wszystkie wymiary, obwód rośnie dwa razy, pole cztery, objętość osiem. „Objętość", która rośnie tylko czterokrotnie, jest w rzeczywistości polem.

Ta kontrola ma granice: (4/3)πr² ma kształt pola, a jednak nie jest polem powierzchni kuli, bo zła jest stała; poprawnie 4πr². Pułapki z zadań: wysokość trójkąta to nie jego bok, tylko odcinek prostopadły do podstawy; koło podane średnicą trzeba najpierw podzielić na pół; ramka szerokości 2 wokół kwadratu 6 daje kwadrat 10, nie 8. Program liczy odpowiedzi dokładnie, jako a + bπ, i zaokrągla dopiero na końcu, a także pokazuje, że pole powierzchni kuli to przyrost objętości na jednostkę promienia.
